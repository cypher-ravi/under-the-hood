Design a News Feed

Build the home feed of a social app: you follow people, they post, and opening the app shows their newest posts first, quickly, page after page. The core decision is when to do the work of assembling a feed: when someone posts (copy the post into every follower's feed) or when someone reads (gather posts from everyone they follow). Accounts with millions of followers break the first; people who follow thousands break the second.

Step order and timings follow Hello Interview's delivery framework. The breakdown on this page is our own.

1

Requirements

about 5 minutes

Keep the feed chronological to start. Ranking with machine learning is a follow-up, and it reuses the same pipeline: it reorders candidates that this design already gathers.

Functional: users should be able to

  • Create a post (text, with optional media).
  • Follow and unfollow other users. Following is one-way and needs no approval.
  • See a feed of posts from everyone they follow, newest first.
  • Scroll back through the feed page by page.

Non-functional: the system should

  • Load a feed page in under about 500 ms.
  • Show a new post to followers within about a minute. Eventual consistency is fine.
  • Favor availability: a slightly stale feed beats an error.
  • Handle billions of users, accounts followed by tens of millions, and users who follow thousands.

First principles: a feed is a merge of many sorted lists (each author's posts, newest first), cut to one page. Every design either does that merge ahead of time and stores the result, or does it on demand. Reads outnumber posts by roughly 100 to 1, which pushes toward doing it ahead of time.

3

API

about 5 minutes
POST /posts                     { body, media_url? }   → { post_id }
PUT  /users/{id}/followers        // follow; DELETE to unfollow
GET  /feed?cursor=&limit=20       → { posts: [...], next_cursor }

// the user comes from the auth token, never from the request body

Why a cursor and not a page number? New posts arrive at the top while you scroll. With ?page=2, everything shifts down and you see repeats. A cursor says "older than this post", which stays correct no matter what arrives. Time-sortable post IDs (a timestamp in the high bits) make the cursor just the last ID you saw.

4

High-level design, then evolve it

10–15 minutes, plus deep dives

Ana has 3 followers. Star has 2 million. Ben follows 300 accounts, including Ana and 4 celebrities like Star. Post as each of them, load Ben's feed, and compare the work each stage does.

5a

Deep dive: merging the hybrid feed, one page at a time

about 5 minutes

In the hybrid design, Ben's page comes from two kinds of source: his precomputed feed (already merged by fan-out) and the recent posts of the few celebrities he follows. Each list is sorted newest first, so the page is a k-way merge with a max-heap that stops after k posts. Numbers are post times; bigger is newer.

The cursor falls out for free: the time of the last post on the page is the next request's "older than" bound. Each source just skips posts at or above it, so page 2 never repeats page 1, even if new posts landed in between.

5b

Deep dive: how much fan-out, and where to draw the celebrity line

about 5 minutes

If everyone posts at a similar rate, the copies made by fan-out on write equal posts × the average number of follows. Celebrities hold a big share of all follow edges, so leaving them out removes that share of the writes, at the price of a few extra reads per feed load.

    Worked example

    Ana posts a photo; Ben opens the app

    Using the final hybrid design. Ana has 3 followers (Ben, Cy and Dee); Ben also follows Star, who has 2 million.

    1. Ana posts

      The post service uploads the photo to blob storage, writes post 9041 to the posts database, and returns. Ana is done in tens of milliseconds; nothing else is in her request path.

    2. Fan-out runs in the background

      A "post created" event goes on a queue. A fan-out worker reads it, checks Ana's follower count (3, well under the celebrity threshold), pages through her followers in the follow graph and prepends 9041 to Ben's, Cy's and Dee's feed lists in the cache. Each list is trimmed to its newest 500 IDs.

    3. Star posts

      Star's follower count is over the threshold, so the worker skips fan-out. The post just sits in the posts database and in a "recent posts by celebrities" cache. Two million writes avoided.

    4. Ben loads his feed

      The feed service reads Ben's cached list (one key), asks the celebrity cache for the recent posts of the 4 celebrities he follows, and merges the five sorted lists into the newest 20 (deep dive 5a). Then it fetches the 20 posts' contents from the post cache in one batch.

    5. Ben scrolls

      The response carries next_cursor = the time of the 20th post. The next request repeats the merge with "older than cursor". If Ben's cached list runs out (he scrolled past 500), the service falls back to fan-out on read for the older range.

    From first principles

    What the problem really is

    What

    A per-user merge of many authors' sorted post lists, cut to one page, served fast.

    Why it's hard

    Follow counts are wildly skewed. The same rule that is cheap for a normal account costs millions of writes for a celebrity, and the rule that is cheap for celebrities costs hundreds of reads per feed load.

    Key insight

    Reads vastly outnumber posts, so precompute, but only where it's cheap. Push for the many small accounts, pull for the few huge ones.

    Where it bends

    Inactive users. Fanning out to someone who hasn't opened the app in months wastes writes; skip them and rebuild their feed on read when they return.

    What's a cache

    The feed lists are derived data. Losing them costs latency, not correctness, because they can be rebuilt from the follow graph and the posts table.

    The story

    The feature everyone hated, then everyone copied

    Facebook launched News Feed on 6 September 2006. Until then you saw a friend's updates only by visiting their profile; now every change was collected on your home page. Many users called it intrusive, and some called for a boycott. Mark Zuckerberg wrote an open letter that began admitting "We really messed this one up", and Facebook added privacy controls. Usage of the feed kept climbing anyway, and it became the template for nearly every social app.

    Twitter faced the same problem in its purest form: a one-way follow graph and a home timeline that is just "tweets from people I follow". In a 2012 QCon San Francisco talk, "Timelines at Scale", Raffi Krikorian described the answer. Each active user's home timeline lived in a Redis cluster, capped at 800 entries. When a tweet arrived, a fan-out service looked up the author's followers and inserted the tweet ID into each of their timelines. Reads were about 300,000 queries a second, against roughly 5,000 new tweets a second on average. For accounts with tens of millions of followers, such as Lady Gaga at the time, fan-out took seconds and could delay delivery, so Twitter worked on merging those tweets in at read time: the hybrid on this page.

    Follow-up questions to expect

    • Ranking: gather a few hundred candidates with this pipeline, score them with a model, then cut to a page. Cursors become opaque tokens because scores aren't times.
    • Unfollow: remove the followee's posts from the feed lazily, by filtering at read time against the current follow set.
    • Deleted or edited posts: feeds store IDs only, and contents are fetched at read time, so an edit shows up everywhere at once. A delete filters out at hydration.
    • A celebrity crosses the threshold: new posts stop fanning out; existing feed entries simply age out.
    • Hot posts: a celebrity's new post is read millions of times in minutes. Replicate it across cache nodes, or serve it from a small local cache in each feed server.
