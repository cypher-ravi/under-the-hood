Design a Parking Lot

A multi-storey car park with entry and exit gates: vehicles of three sizes arrive, get a ticket and a spot, leave, and pay by the hour, while a board at the entrance shows what's free. Nothing here is about servers or scale. The interviewer wants clean classes with one job each, the right relationships between them, a few patterns used for a reason, and code that stays correct when two gates hand out the last spot at the same moment.

The interview flow is adapted from Hello Interview's delivery framework for a 45-minute round. The breakdown, diagram and code on this page are our own.

1

Requirements

about 5 minutes

Ask until the scope fits in 45 minutes. Payments by card, reservations, licence-plate cameras and many lots across a city are follow-ups, not the core.

Functional: the lot should

  • Have several floors, each with spots of three sizes: small, compact and large.
  • Accept three vehicle types: bike, car, truck. A vehicle fits its own size or any bigger spot.
  • Issue a ticket at an entry gate (vehicle, spot, entry time), or turn the vehicle away when nothing fits.
  • At an exit gate, free the spot and charge per started hour, with a rate per vehicle size.
  • Show free spots per size on a display board, updated on every change.

Non-functional: the code should

  • Be thread-safe: several gates run at once and never hand the same spot to two vehicles.
  • Be extensible: a new vehicle type, spot type or pricing rule means adding a class, not editing the lot.
  • Find a spot in O(1)-ish time: no scan over thousands of spots on every arrival.
  • Keep the domain testable without threads, clocks or hardware (time is passed in).

First principles: say out loud what you are not building. An LLD round rewards a small, correct core with clear seams for later, more than a sprawling model with twenty half-designed classes.

2

Core entities and their responsibilities

about 3 minutes

Pull the nouns out of the requirements, then give each one a single job. If a class's job needs the word "and", it may be two classes.

ParkingLot

owns floors and the free-spot index; parks and releases vehicles atomically; tells observers what changed

Floor

a numbered level that holds its spots

Spot

floor, number, size; "0-2" is floor 0, spot 2

Vehicle

plate and size; Bike, Car, Truck are subclasses

Ticket

id, vehicle, spot, entry time; the proof of what was parked where

Gate

an entry or exit point; turns a request into a call on the lot

AssignmentStrategy

chooses which free spot a vehicle gets

PricingStrategy

turns size and minutes parked into a fee

Display

the "spaces free" board; listens for changes

Class diagram

about 6 minutes

The lot owns its floors and floors own their spots: if the lot goes away, so do they (composition, filled diamond). Vehicles are not owned; a ticket only points at one (association). The two strategies and the observer are interfaces, so the lot depends on what they promise, not on which class keeps it.

The whole diagram, scaled to fit. Zoom in to read the members, then scroll sideways.
inherits from (is a) implements an interface composition (owns, same lifetime) association (knows about) dependency (uses, creates) 1..*multiplicity: how many at that end

Reading it: "ParkingLot ◆— 1..* Floor" says one lot is made of one or more floors. "ParkingLot ◆— 0..* Ticket" says the lot holds zero or more active tickets. "1" next to an interface says the lot uses exactly one strategy at a time. Italic names are abstract: you never create a plain Vehicle.

Interfaces of the main classes

about 5 minutes

Write the signatures before any bodies. They are the contract the rest of the interview builds on, and they show where the seams are.

class ParkingLot:
    __init__(floors: list[list[Size]], strategy: AssignmentStrategy,
             pricing: PricingStrategy)
    park(vehicle: Vehicle, now: int) -> Ticket | None   # None = nothing fits
    leave(ticket_id: str, now: int) -> int              # the fee
    subscribe(observer: LotObserver) -> None

class Gate:
    enter(kind: str, plate: str, now: int) -> Ticket | None
    exit(ticket: Ticket, now: int) -> int

interface AssignmentStrategy:
    pick(free: dict[Size, heap], size: Size) -> Spot | None
interface PricingStrategy:
    fee(size: Size, minutes: int) -> int
interface LotObserver:
    update(free_counts: dict[Size, int]) -> None

class VehicleFactory:
    create(kind: str, plate: str) -> Vehicle             # "car" -> Car

# time is minutes since midnight, passed in: tests never sleep or mock a clock

Why park returns None instead of raising: a full lot is a normal outcome at 9 am, not an error. Keep exceptions for bugs, like a ticket id that was never issued.

5

Design patterns, and why

about 5 minutes

Name a pattern only when it solves a problem you can point at in the requirements. Each card says where it earns its place and where it would be ceremony.

Strategy: spot assignment and pricing

Both are rules the business will change without touching the lot: "nearest first" today, "save large spots for trucks" tomorrow; hourly on weekdays, first hour free at weekends. Each rule is one small class behind one method, chosen when the lot is built.

Not worth it when there is truly one rule and no hint of a second. A plain function is fine; extract the interface the day a second rule arrives.

Observer: display boards

The lot shouldn't know how many boards exist, or that one is an LED sign and another a mobile app. It keeps a list of LotObservers and calls update after each change. New listeners (analytics, an "almost full" alert) subscribe without editing the lot.

Watch out: call observers outside the lock, or a slow board stalls every gate. With one listener and no plans for more, a direct call is simpler.

Factory: vehicles (and tickets)

Gates receive a string from the barrier hardware ("car") and need the right subclass. One registry maps kinds to classes, so adding a van is one entry. Ticket ids also come from one place (a counter inside the lot), so two gates can't mint the same id.

Not worth it as a class hierarchy of factories. A dictionary of constructors is the whole pattern here; an AbstractVehicleFactoryFactory is a red flag.

Singleton: the lot (usually a smell)

"There is only one lot" tempts a ParkingLot.get_instance(). That hides a global: every class reaches for it, tests can't build a small two-spot lot, and a second site means a rewrite.

Better: create one lot at startup and pass it in (dependency injection), as Gate(name, lot) does. You still have one instance, without the global. Mention Singleton to show you know it, then explain why you didn't use it.

    Code
    about 7 minutes
    
    
    Runnable Python 3, standard library only. The core classes come first, then a short demo that replays the walkthrough's morning under both strategies and races two threads for one spot. In an interview you'd write ParkingLot.park, leave and one strategy in full, and sketch the rest.
    

    import heapq, itertools, math, threading
from abc import ABC, abstractmethod
from collections import namedtuple
from enum import IntEnum

Size = IntEnum("Size", "SMALL COMPACT LARGE")   # bigger spots fit smaller vehicles

class Vehicle:
    size: Size                      # each subclass fixes its size
    def __init__(self, plate): self.plate = plate
class Bike(Vehicle): size = Size.SMALL
class Car(Vehicle): size = Size.COMPACT
class Truck(Vehicle): size = Size.LARGE

class VehicleFactory:
    kinds = {"bike": Bike, "car": Car, "truck": Truck}   # new type = one new entry
    @classmethod
    def create(cls, kind, plate): return cls.kinds[kind](plate)

class Spot:
    def __init__(self, floor, number, size):
        self.floor, self.number, self.size = floor, number, size
        self.id = f"{floor}-{number}"

class Floor:
    def __init__(self, number, sizes):
        self.number = number
        self.spots = [Spot(number, n, size) for n, size in enumerate(sizes)]

Ticket = namedtuple("Ticket", "id vehicle spot entered")

class AssignmentStrategy(ABC):      # free[size]: min-heap of (floor, number, spot)
    @abstractmethod
    def pick(self, free, size): ...
class NearestFirst(AssignmentStrategy):
    def pick(self, free, size):     # lowest floor/number among spots that fit
        heads = [free[s][0] for s in Size if s >= size and free[s]]
        return min(heads, key=lambda h: h[:2])[-1] if heads else None
class BestFit(AssignmentStrategy):
    def pick(self, free, size):     # smallest size that fits; saves large spots
        return next((free[s][0][-1] for s in Size if s >= size and free[s]), None)

class PricingStrategy(ABC):
    @abstractmethod
    def fee(self, size, minutes): ...
class Hourly(PricingStrategy):
    def __init__(self, rates): self.rates = rates
    def fee(self, size, minutes):   # every started hour is billed, at least one
        return max(1, math.ceil(minutes / 60)) * self.rates[size]
class FirstHourFree(Hourly):
    def fee(self, size, minutes):
        return max(0, math.ceil(minutes / 60) - 1) * self.rates[size]

class LotObserver(ABC):
    @abstractmethod
    def update(self, free_counts): ...
class Display(LotObserver):         # the "spaces free" board at the entrance
    def __init__(self): self.text = ""
    def update(self, free_counts):
        self.text = " ".join(f"{s.name[0]}{n}" for s, n in free_counts.items())

class ParkingLot:
    def __init__(self, floors, strategy, pricing):
        self.strategy, self.pricing = strategy, pricing
        self.floors = [Floor(f, sizes) for f, sizes in enumerate(floors)]
        self.free = {s: [] for s in Size}
        for spot in (sp for fl in self.floors for sp in fl.spots):
            heapq.heappush(self.free[spot.size], (spot.floor, spot.number, spot))
        self.tickets, self.observers = {}, []
        self.ids = itertools.count(1)
        self.lock = threading.Lock()
    def subscribe(self, observer):
        self.observers.append(observer)
        observer.update(self.counts())
    def counts(self):
        return {s: len(heap) for s, heap in self.free.items()}
    def park(self, vehicle, now):
        with self.lock:             # find and take a spot as one atomic step
            spot = self.strategy.pick(self.free, vehicle.size)
            if spot is None:
                return None
            heapq.heappop(self.free[spot.size])   # strategies only pick heap heads
            ticket = Ticket(f"T{next(self.ids)}", vehicle, spot, now)
            self.tickets[ticket.id] = ticket
            counts = self.counts()
        for o in self.observers: o.update(counts)   # notify outside the lock
        return ticket
    def leave(self, ticket_id, now):
        with self.lock:
            t = self.tickets.pop(ticket_id)
            s = t.spot
            heapq.heappush(self.free[s.size], (s.floor, s.number, s))
            counts = self.counts()
        for o in self.observers: o.update(counts)
        return self.pricing.fee(t.vehicle.size, now - t.entered)

class Gate:
    def __init__(self, name, lot): self.name, self.lot = name, lot
    def enter(self, kind, plate, now):
        return self.lot.park(VehicleFactory.create(kind, plate), now)
    def exit(self, ticket, now): return self.lot.leave(ticket.id, now)

# ---------------- demo ----------------
RATES = {Size.SMALL: 1, Size.COMPACT: 3, Size.LARGE: 5}
FLOORS = [[Size.SMALL, Size.COMPACT, Size.LARGE], [Size.COMPACT, Size.LARGE]]
EVENTS = [("08:00", "E1", "car", "KA-01"), ("08:05", "E2", "car", "KA-02"),
          ("08:10", "E1", "truck", "TR-07"), ("08:20", "E2", "bike", "BK-11"),
          ("08:40", "E1", "truck", "TR-08"), ("09:35", "X1", "out", "KA-01"),
          ("10:30", "X1", "out", "TR-07"), ("10:45", "E2", "truck", "TR-09"),
          ("11:50", "X1", "out", "BK-11")]
minutes = lambda hhmm: int(hhmm[:2]) * 60 + int(hhmm[3:])

def run(strategy):
    lot, board = ParkingLot(FLOORS, strategy, Hourly(RATES)), Display()
    lot.subscribe(board)
    gates = {g: Gate(g, lot) for g in ("E1", "E2", "X1")}
    print(f"== {type(strategy).__name__} + Hourly ==")
    held = {}                       # plate -> ticket (the stub the driver keeps)
    for at, gate, kind, plate in EVENTS:
        now = minutes(at)
        if kind == "out":
            t = held.pop(plate)
            fee = gates[gate].exit(t, now)
            what = f"{plate} leaves {t.spot.id}, {now - t.entered} min, ${fee}"
        else:
            t = gates[gate].enter(kind, plate, now)
            if t: held[plate] = t
            what = f"{kind} {plate} -> {f'{t.id} at {t.spot.id}' if t else 'FULL'}"
        print(f"{at} {gate}  {what:<31} board {board.text}")

run(NearestFirst())
run(BestFit())

# two gates race for the last spot
tiny, results = ParkingLot([[Size.COMPACT]], NearestFirst(), Hourly(RATES)), []
start = threading.Barrier(2)
def arrive(gate):
    start.wait()                    # both gates press "issue ticket" together
    results.append(Gate(gate, tiny).enter("car", gate + "-car", 0))
threads = [threading.Thread(target=arrive, args=(g,)) for g in ("E1", "E2")]
for th in threads: th.start()
for th in threads: th.join()
won = sum(r is not None for r in results)
print(f"race for 1 spot: {won} parked, {len(results) - won} turned away")

    Why heaps: free[size] is a min-heap ordered by (floor, number), one per size. A strategy only looks at the top of at most three heaps, and taking or returning a spot is O(log n). With thousands of spots that is a handful of comparisons, not a scan. The board's counts are just the heap lengths.
    
  
    

  
    
    
    8a
    Deep dive: two gates, one last spot
    about 4 minutes
    
    
    Parking is check-then-act: look for a free spot, then take it. If two gates both look before either takes, both see the same spot. Below, gates A and B run park for one remaining spot, 1-1. The schedule says which gate runs its next line; turn the lock off and on.
    
    
    
    
    Three ways to make it safe
    
    
      
      
    • One lock around the lot (what the code does). Simplest, and plenty: a gate holds it for microseconds while a car takes seconds to pass the barrier. Ten gates never queue in practice.
      • 
      
    • Finer locks, per floor or per size. More parallelism, more risk. NearestFirst may compare heads across sizes, so it would need several locks at once; always take them in the same order (small, compact, large) or two gates can deadlock waiting on each other.
      • 
      
    • Compare-and-set on the spot. No lock held while choosing: pick a candidate, then atomically flip it from free to taken only if it is still free; if that fails, pick again. This is what a database does with UPDATE spots SET ticket = ? WHERE id = ? AND ticket IS NULL and a check that one row changed. It scales across processes, which a threading.Lock cannot.
      • 
    
    
    
    Keep the critical section small: only the pick and the take go inside the lock. Computing the fee and notifying observers happen after it is released, so a slow display board never blocks a gate.
    
  
    

  
    
    
    8b
    Deep dive: what changes when requirements land
    about 3 minutes
    
    
    The test of the design is how many existing lines a new requirement edits. Open/closed: open to new classes, closed to edits of the ones that work.
    
    
    
      
    New requirementAddEdit
    
      
        
    Vans (compact or large?)class Van(Vehicle)one factory entry
    
        
    Weekend flat rateclass WeekendFlat(PricingStrategy)nothing
    
        
    Peak-hour surcharge on any ruleclass Surcharge(PricingStrategy)nothing (it wraps another)
    
        
    Disabled-badge spots near the lifta strategy that prefers themSpot gains a feature
    
        
    EV charging spotsElectricCar, EVFirst, ChargingFeefree index keyed by (size, feature)
    
          
    
    
    
    EV charging is the honest stress test. A charger is a feature of a spot, not a fourth size: an EV can still park in a plain compact spot, and a petrol car could take a charging spot (you'd rather it didn't). So Spot gets a set of features, the free index is keyed by (size, feature), a new EVFirst strategy tries charging spots before plain ones, and a ChargingFee pricing wrapper adds energy used on top of the hourly fee. The lot's park and leave stay as they are. Admit the one edit: the index shape changes, because we chose to key it by size alone.
    
  
    

  
    
    
    8c
    Deep dive: SOLID, and where to stop
    about 2 minutes
    
    
    
      
    Single responsibility
    Pricing, choosing spots and drawing boards each live in their own class. The lot still does two things: allocating spots and keeping tickets. Splitting out a TicketService is reasonable once tickets gain payments and lost-ticket handling; before that it adds a class with nothing in it.
    
      
    Open/closed
    New rules arrive as new strategy classes (see 8b). The cost: a reader must look in several files to see the full behavior.
    
      
    Liskov
    FirstHourFree extends Hourly and keeps its contract: same inputs, a fee that is never negative. A subclass that raised on short stays would break callers that trusted the parent.
    
      
    Interface segregation
    Observers get one method, update. A board never sees park or the lock.
    
      
    Dependency inversion
    The lot depends on AssignmentStrategy and PricingStrategy, not on BestFit or Hourly. That is what lets the walkthrough swap them with a dropdown.
    
      
    Where to stop
    No interface for Spot, Floor or Ticket: they are plain data with no second implementation in sight. Abstractions cost reading time; spend them where change is likely.

    From first principles
    
    What the problem really is
    
    
    
      
    What
    A pool of typed resources (spots), handed out to typed requests (vehicles) by a replaceable rule, returned later with a bill computed by another replaceable rule.
    
      
    Why it's hard
    Not the algorithms. It's choosing boundaries that survive change, and making "find a free one and take it" a single atomic step.
    
      
    Key insight
    Keep a ready index of free spots instead of scanning, and put the two rules that change most (where to park, what to charge) behind interfaces.
    
      
    Same shape elsewhere
    Seats in a cinema, rooms in a hotel, desks in an office, connections in a pool: allocate from typed inventory, release, charge or log.
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    
    
      
    Follow-up questions to expect
    
      
      
        
    • Lost ticket: look up the active ticket by plate (keep a plate → ticket map), and charge a daily maximum if no entry time can be proven.
      • 
        
    • Payment methods: cash, card or app at the exit, or pay at a kiosk before walking back. A Payment with a status, and the exit gate opens only for a paid ticket.
      • 
        
    • Reservations: a spot can be held for a time window, so "free" becomes "free between 10:00 and 12:00". The free index now has to answer time-range questions.
      • 
        
    • A bus that needs five large spots in a row: the strategy must find a run of adjacent free spots on one floor, which a per-size heap can't answer; keep a per-floor bitmap of free spots for that.
      • 
        
    • A board per floor: pass floor-level counts to observers, and let each board filter for its floor.
      • 
        
    • The process crashes: in-memory state is gone. Persist tickets (and which spots they hold) before opening the barrier, and rebuild the heaps from the open tickets at startup.
      • 
        
    • Many lots across a city: that's a high-level design question: a service per site, a central booking API, and the compare-and-set from 8a moved into the database.
