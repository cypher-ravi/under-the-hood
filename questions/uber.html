Design Uber

Build a ride-hailing service: a rider asks for a ride, nearby drivers are found in seconds, one of them accepts, and nobody gets two rides at once. Two forces shape the design. Drivers report their position every few seconds, which is a firehose of tiny writes. And matching is a race: many riders want the same nearest driver at the same moment.

Step order and timings follow Hello Interview's delivery framework. The breakdown on this page is our own.

1

Requirements

about 5 minutes

Scope to the ride itself. Payments, ratings, pooled rides and scheduled rides are follow-ups.

Functional: users should be able to

  • Riders: enter a pickup and destination and see a fare estimate.
  • Riders: request a ride and be matched with a nearby available driver.
  • Drivers: go online, share their location, and accept or decline ride offers.
  • Both: see the ride's status and the driver's position until pickup.

Non-functional: the system should

  • Match a rider within about a minute, or say no driver was found.
  • Be consistent about assignment: a driver is never given two rides at once.
  • Absorb millions of location updates a second, and surges near events.
  • Stay available for location updates; a few seconds of staleness is fine.

First principles: notice the two different consistency needs. Driver locations can be slightly stale (a car moves maybe 50 metres between updates), so favor availability and speed. Assignment must be exact, so it needs a lock or a transaction. Splitting those paths is most of the design.

about 5 minutes

Why a separate fare step? The price must be the one the rider saw. Creating a Fare with an expiry, then requesting a ride by fare_id, stops a client from sending its own price. Offers reach drivers over a connection the driver app already keeps open for location updates, with a mobile push as a backup.

4

High-level design, then evolve it

10–15 minutes, plus deep dives

A toy city: 20 km × 20 km with 5,000 online drivers, and riders want someone within 2 km. Three drivers sit near the rider. Send pings, request rides (one rider, or two at the same moment), accept, and compare what each stage costs and where it breaks.

5a

Deep dive: offering a ride without double-booking

about 5 minutes

The location index returns candidates sorted by distance. The matching service walks that list, takes a short lock on a driver before offering, and releases it if they decline or don't answer. The lock (a key with a 10-second expiry, set only if absent) is what stops two riders' requests from grabbing the same driver.

Why the lock expires on its own: if the matching server crashes mid-offer, nobody will ever delete the lock. The TTL frees the driver anyway. In this run a decline takes 3 seconds, an accept 4, and no answer costs the full 10-second timeout.

5b

Deep dive: sizing the location firehose

about 5 minutes

Location updates dwarf everything else. Each one is tiny and only the latest per driver matters, so keep them in memory, overwrite in place, and expire drivers who go silent. Ride requests are rarer but bursty, and each one is a geo query.

    Worked example

    Ana leaves a concert

    Using the final design. It's 11 pm, the venue empties, and requests in that part of the city jump tenfold.

    1. Fare first

      Ana's app sends pickup and destination. The ride service computes a route and price, stores fare 7712 with a two-minute expiry, and shows her the price.

    2. Request

      POST /rides {fare_id: 7712} writes a ride row with status matching and puts a match job on the queue for her region. Ana gets an immediate "finding your driver". The queue absorbs the burst; matching workers drain it at their own pace.

    3. Find candidates

      A matching worker asks the location index for drivers near the pickup. It checks the geohash cell holding the pickup and the 8 around it, keeps available drivers updated in the last 30 seconds, and sorts by distance (in production, by estimated pickup time): D1 at 0.4 km, D2 at 0.9 km, D3 at 1.3 km.

    4. Lock and offer

      The worker tries SET lock:D1 ride-9 NX EX 10. Another rider's job already holds it, so the set fails and the worker moves on. D2's lock succeeds, the offer goes out over D2's open connection, and D2 declines after 3 seconds. The worker deletes the lock and tries D3, who accepts.

    5. Assign

      The ride row is updated to driver_id = D3, status = accepted in one conditional write ("only if status is still matching"), and D3's status becomes on_trip. Ana's app switches to the live map, fed by D3's location updates.

    From first principles

    What the problem really is

    What

    A continuously updated map of free drivers, a fast "who's near this point?" query, and an exclusive handshake between one rider and one driver.

    Why it's hard

    Two opposite workloads share one system: a huge stream of disposable location writes, and a small stream of matches that must never conflict.

    Key insight

    Split them. Locations live in memory, are overwritten, and are allowed to be a little stale. Assignment goes through a lock with an expiry and a conditional write.

    Where it bends

    Dense downtown cells hold thousands of drivers while rural cells hold none. Adapt the search radius, or use an index with finer cells where it's busy (a quadtree, or several geohash precisions).

    What's derived

    The location index is rebuilt from pings within seconds if a node dies. The rides database is the source of truth and must be durable.

    The story

    From a black-car app to a hexagon map of the planet

    Uber started as UberCab, an idea Garrett Camp and Travis Kalanick worked out in 2008: tap a phone, get a black car. It began offering rides in San Francisco in 2010. The early dispatch system was simple, and it grew city by city until the company rebuilt it.

    At QCon London in March 2015, Matt Ranney, Uber's chief systems architect, described the rewrite. The dispatch system was written almost entirely in Node.js. Drivers sent their location about every 4 seconds. To answer "who is near this rider?", the earth was divided into cells with Google's S2 library, using level-12 cells of roughly 3 to 6 square kilometres. To spread that state across many servers, Uber built Ringpop: a consistent hash ring whose members find each other with the SWIM gossip protocol, so any server can tell which one owns a given cell.

    In 2018 Uber open-sourced H3, its own grid of hexagons. Hexagons have one useful property squares lack: every neighbour's center is the same distance away. H3 has 16 resolutions, each cell about a seventh of the area of its parent, and Uber computes surge pricing from supply and demand inside these hexagons.

    Follow-up questions to expect

    • Reducing pings: send updates less often when the car is parked or the driver is on a trip far from any rider, and more often when moving in a busy area. Fewer writes, same matching quality.
    • Better matching: rank by estimated time to pickup on the road network, not straight-line distance. Batch requests over a second or two and solve the assignment for the group.
    • Surge pricing: count requests and free drivers per cell (H3 hexagons, for example) over a short window and raise the price where demand outruns supply.
    • A matching server dies mid-offer: the lock expires on its own, the job is still on the queue (acknowledged only after assignment), and another worker picks it up.
    • Global scale: shard everything by region. A ride never crosses cities, so locks, queues and location indexes stay local.
