Geohash and Quadtrees

"Find drivers within 2 km" can't use an ordinary index. A B-tree sorts by one number, and a location is two. Both techniques here turn 2D space into something you can index: a geohash turns a point into a string whose prefix means "nearby", and a quadtree keeps splitting busy areas into four until each box holds only a few points.

Worked example

Encoding a point in Bengaluru, bit by bit

The point is latitude 12.9716, longitude 77.5946. A geohash halves the world again and again, alternating longitude and latitude, and writes 1 when the point is in the upper half.

  1. Group bits in fives and encode in base 32

    Bits 1–5 are 11001 = 25, which is t in the alphabet 0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz (a, i, l and o are left out to avoid confusion). Bits 6–10 are 01100 = 12, which is d. Continuing gives tdr1v9.

  2. Each character narrows the box

    Every character adds 5 bits and shrinks the cell by 32 times. At the equator, 4 characters is about 39 × 20 km, 5 is about 4.9 × 4.9 km, 6 is about 1.2 × 0.6 km, and 7 is about 153 × 153 m.

  3. Shared prefix means same box

    Every point inside the 5-character cell tdr1v has a geohash starting with tdr1v. So "everything in this cell" is a simple prefix search, which an ordinary database index handles: WHERE geohash LIKE 'tdr1v%'.

  4. The catch: edges

    The points at longitude 77.6069 and 77.6079 (same latitude) are only about 108 m apart, but they fall on either side of a cell edge: tdr1v and tdr1y. Meanwhile two opposite corners of tdr1v are almost 7 km apart. A shared prefix proves closeness, but a different prefix doesn't prove distance. That's why real searches look at the rider's cell and its 8 neighbours, then filter by exact distance.

  5. The quadtree alternative

    A quadtree starts with one box for the whole city. When a box holds more than a set number of drivers, say 4, it splits into four quarters and hands its drivers down. Busy downtown ends up with tiny boxes, and empty outskirts stay as one big box. A search visits only the boxes that overlap the search circle.

The story

Squeezing two dimensions into one

The core trick is older than the web. In 1966, G. M. Morton at IBM described a way to file geographic records for a geodetic database. He interleaved the bits of the two coordinates into one number, so records close on the map tended to sit close together in the file. The path this ordering traces through space zigzags like the letter Z, and it's now called the Z-order or Morton curve.

In 1974, Raphael Finkel and Jon Bentley named the quadtree in a paper on retrieving records by composite keys. Instead of a fixed grid, it adapts: split any region that gets too crowded into four, recursively. Games, image compression and map tiles all came to use the same idea.

In February 2008, Gustavo Niemeyer launched geohash.org and placed the geohash in the public domain. It was the Z-order idea again, apparently reinvented independently, with the bits written in a readable base-32 string. The string form made it easy to store in any database and to share as a short link to a place.

From first principles

Rebuilding the idea from the problem

What

Ways to index points on a map so "what's near here?" touches a small, nearby part of the data instead of all of it.

Why

Indexes sort by one key. Sorting by latitude alone finds a thin strip around the world, not a circle around you.

When

The 1960s and 70s for the underlying ideas. Geohash came in 2008, just as phones with GPS made "near me" queries common.

Where

IBM (Morton's file ordering), academic computer science (Finkel and Bentley), and an independent developer (Niemeyer's geohash.org).

How

Geohash: interleave the bits so nearby points share a prefix, then search the cell and its neighbours. Quadtree: split crowded boxes recursively, then search only the boxes that overlap the circle.

The one-line insight: turn "close in two dimensions" into "close in one ordering" (geohash), or keep the space as a tree whose shape follows the data (quadtree).

The trade-off: geohash cells are fixed, so a crowded cell holds too many points and an empty one wastes a lookup. Quadtrees adapt to density, but must be rebalanced as points move.

What to notice

  • With a 2 km radius, precision 6 has cells smaller than the circle, so the 3 × 3 block can't cover it. Red drivers are missed. Pick a precision whose cell is at least as big as the radius.
  • At precision 4 nothing is missed, but almost every driver is checked. Too coarse is correct but slow.
  • Add a stadium crowd. The geohash cell over the stadium balloons in candidates, while the quadtree splits that area into many small boxes.
  • Move the rider near a cell edge in geohash mode. The rider's own cell covers little of the circle, and the neighbours do most of the work.

Interview angle

Which for a ride-hailing app? Drivers report their location every few seconds. Updating a geohash is one string change, which suits Redis or a database index. A quadtree must move points between nodes and occasionally re-split, so it usually lives in memory on dedicated servers.

Which for a restaurant search? Restaurants rarely move and density varies a lot, so a quadtree or R-tree built once works well.

Always: search the neighbouring cells as well, then filter candidates by true distance.
