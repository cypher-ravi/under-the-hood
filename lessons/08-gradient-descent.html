08 · Gradient descent: walking downhill on the loss

You can't see the whole loss landscape, but you can always feel which way the ground tilts where you stand. Gradient descent takes a small step downhill, feels again, and repeats. That loop, run billions of times, is how almost every modern model is trained.

Math you'll need
  • M05 · Partial derivatives and the gradient

Rusty on this? It's a short, from-scratch math lesson. Read it first, then come back.

a · Where we areFrom a bowl to a recipe for reaching its bottom

Lesson 03 fitted the line ŷ = w·x + b to five flats by trying 24,381 knob settings. Lesson 05 showed that a slope tells you which way is downhill. Lesson 07 gave us the thing to go downhill on: the mean squared error, a bowl over the knobs with its lowest point, 0.38, at w = 2.15, b = 5.6. Today we join them into the algorithm that does the training: gradient descent.

b · Why this mattersThis loop is "training"

Grid search worked for two knobs. A language model has billions, and a grid with just 10 values per knob would need more settings than there are atoms in the universe. Gradient descent doesn't care: each step costs about one pass over the data, however many knobs there are. When you see a PyTorch loop with loss.backward() followed by optimizer.step(), the first line computes the slopes and the second line takes the step you're about to learn. Every knob in the model gets nudged downhill, every step. Understanding the learning rate, which this lesson is mostly about, is also the first thing you tune when training goes wrong.

c · The intuitionA hillside in thick fog

You're standing on a hillside in fog so thick you can see only your own boots. Somewhere below is the valley floor and you want to reach it. You can't see it, so you can't walk straight to it. But you can feel the ground: is it tilting down to the left or the right, and how steeply?

So you do the obvious thing. Feel the tilt, take a step in the downhill direction, feel again, step again. Where it's steep you are clearly far from the bottom, so a bigger stride is fine. As the ground flattens, your strides get shorter. When it's flat in every direction, you stop: you're at the bottom.

That's gradient descent. The hillside is the loss, your position is the knob settings, and "feeling the tilt" is computing the slope (Lesson 05). The one choice you have to make is how big a stride to take for a given tilt. Too timid and you'll be walking all night; too bold and you'll leap clean over the valley and land higher up the other side.

d · How it actually worksSeven steps, from one knob to billions

1. One knob: feel the slope

Start simple. Hold b at 5.6 and let only w move. The loss is then a function of one number, Lesson 07's bowl:

L(w) = (1/5)·Σ(w·xᵢ + 5.6 − yᵢ)²

We need its slope, dL/dw. Lesson 05 found it for flat C alone: (8w − 22)² has slope 128w − 352, which is the same as 2 · (8w − 22) · 8, that is, 2 × error × x. (M05 shows why this shortcut always works.) The same holds for every flat, and the slope of an average is the average of the slopes, so:

dL/dw = (2/5)·Σ errorᵢ·xᵢ

where errorᵢ = w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ is flat i's guess minus its real rent.

Stand at w = 3 (with b = 5.6, each error is 3x + 5.6 − y) and compute:

So dL/dw = (2/5) × 306 = 122.4, and the loss here is 52.4. Read it like Lesson 05: positive, so increasing w makes things worse and downhill is to the left; large, so the ground is steep and we're far from the bottom. Every error is positive too: this line prices every flat too high.

2. The update rule

Now take a step against the slope:

w ← w − η · dL/dw
η ("eta"), the learning rate
A small positive number you choose, which turns "how steep" into "how far to move". Lesson 05 called it the step size. It's a hyperparameter: a setting of the training process, not a knob the model learns.
Why the minus sign
The slope points uphill. Subtracting it moves you downhill whichever way that is: a positive slope makes w smaller, a negative slope makes it bigger.
"←"
Read as "becomes", like w = w - lr * dw in code.

With η = 0.005: the move is −0.005 × 122.4 = −0.612, so w becomes 3 − 0.612 = 2.388. The loss there is 4.4584, down from 52.4 in one step.

Notice we never looked at the rest of the curve. One slope, computed from where we stand, was enough to pick the direction.

4. The learning rate: too small, about right, too big

Everything above used η = 0.005. Change it and the same walk looks completely different. Here are ten steps from w = 3 with four learning rates:

Too small · η = 0.001
About right · η = 0.005
Big: bounces · η = 0.012
Too big · η = 0.015

Same bowl, same start, ten steps each. Small η crawls; η = 0.005 is already at the bottom; η = 0.012 hops from side to side but closes in; η = 0.015 overshoots further every time.

For this bowl we can see exactly why. Its slope works out to dL/dw = 144 · (w − 2.15): 144 times your distance from the bottom. Put that into the rule and the distance after a step is the distance before, times (1 − 144η):

  • η = 0.001: × 0.856. Each step removes only 14% of the gap. Safe, slow.
  • η = 0.005: × 0.28. Each step removes 72% of the gap.
  • η = 0.012: × −0.728. The minus sign means you land on the other side, but closer.
  • η = 0.015: × −1.16. You land on the other side and further away, every step. The loss explodes: 1,012.7 after ten steps. This is divergence.

The tipping point is η = 2/144 ≈ 0.0139. Real models have no tidy formula like this, so people try a few learning rates (say 0.1, 0.01, 0.001) and watch the loss: falling slowly means go bigger, jumping around or growing means go smaller.

5. Two knobs: follow the gradient

Now free b as well. The loss is a surface over the (w, b) plane, and at each point we need two slopes: one for nudging w with b held still, one the other way round. These are partial derivatives, and listed together they form the gradient (Lesson 05 met it briefly; M05 builds it up from scratch):

∇L = ( ∂L/∂w , ∂L/∂b )
∂L/∂w = (2/5)·Σ errorᵢ·xᵢ
∂L/∂b = (2/5)·Σ errorᵢ

The gradient points straight uphill, the steepest way up. Gradient descent steps the opposite way, both knobs at once, with one learning rate:

w ← w − η·∂L/∂w
b ← b − η·∂L/∂b

Start at Lesson 07's line ŷ = 3x (w = 3, b = 0, loss 7.6). The errors are −2, −1, 2, 2, 5; they add to 6, and error × x adds to 82. So ∇L = (0.4 × 82, 0.4 × 6) = (32.8, 2.4). With η = 0.01: w = 3 − 0.328 = 2.672, b = 0 − 0.024 = −0.024, and the loss drops to 4.6566.

Look at b: the best value is 5.6, yet the first step moved it down. The gradient points downhill from where you stand, not at the bottom. Here the bowl is a long, narrow valley, and the steepest way down runs mostly across it, so the first steps fix the fast direction and the slow slide along the valley comes after.

6. When to stop

The rule never says "done", so you decide. Three common tests, often used together:

  • The ground is flat: the gradient's length, √((∂L/∂w)² + (∂L/∂b)²), falls below a small number like 0.001. At the bottom both slopes are 0, so a nearly flat gradient means you're nearly there.
  • The loss stops improving: it drops by less than some tiny amount over the last many steps.
  • A budget: a fixed number of steps, or hours of compute. Big models are mostly trained this way.

That long tail is typical: early steps buy a lot, later steps buy very little. It's why training curves are usually drawn with the loss falling steeply then flattening, and why "train a bit longer" has diminishing returns.

7. Why not just search?

Lesson 03's grid checked 24,381 settings. Gradient descent reached the same answer, w = 2.15, b = 5.6 to the grid's precision, in 2,198 steps, and each step costs about the same as one check: one pass over the five flats.

For two knobs that's a nice saving. With more knobs it's the whole game. A grid with 100 values per knob needs 100k checks for k knobs: 10,000 for 2 knobs, 1020 for 10, impossible for a million. Gradient descent needs k slopes per step, and backpropagation (Lesson 23) gets all of them in about the cost of two passes through the model.

Three honest caveats:

  • One bottom here, many elsewhere. MSE for a line is always one smooth bowl. Neural network losses are bumpy, with many dips, and gradient descent finds a low point, a local minimum, not a guaranteed lowest one. In practice that works remarkably well.
  • Narrow valleys are slow. Most of those 2,198 steps were spent crawling along the valley. Rescaling the inputs fixes most of that: measure area from 800 sq ft instead of from 0 and the valley becomes nearly round, and 49 steps (with η = 0.1) reach the bottom.
  • Real training uses a sample per step. Instead of all the examples, each step looks at a random handful: stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent. Lessons 24 and 25 cover those and smarter optimizers.

The same thing in plain Python: the whole of gradient descent is the few lines inside each loop.

# the five flats from Lesson 03: x = area (100s of sq ft), y = rent (Rs 1000s)
X = [4, 6, 8, 10, 12]
Y = [14, 19, 22, 28, 31]

def loss(w, b):
    """mean squared error (Lesson 07)"""
    return sum((w * x + b - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(X, Y)) / len(X)

def gradient(w, b):
    """slopes of the loss: (dL/dw, dL/db)"""
    errors = [w * x + b - y for x, y in zip(X, Y)]
    dw = 2 * sum(e * x for e, x in zip(errors, X)) / len(X)
    db = 2 * sum(errors) / len(X)
    return dw, db

# 1. one knob: hold b at 5.6, start at w = 3, learning rate 0.005
w, b, lr = 3.0, 5.6, 0.005
for step in range(1, 6):
    dw, _ = gradient(w, b)
    w = w - lr * dw
    print(step, round(dw, 4), round(w, 4), round(loss(w, b), 4))

# 2. same start, 10 steps, four learning rates
for lr in [0.001, 0.005, 0.012, 0.015]:
    w = 3.0
    for step in range(10):
        w = w - lr * gradient(w, 5.6)[0]
    print(lr, round(w, 4), round(loss(w, 5.6), 4))

# 3. two knobs, start at y = 3x (w = 3, b = 0), learning rate 0.01
def descend(lr, w=3.0, b=0.0, tol=0.001, max_steps=100_000):
    for step in range(max_steps):
        dw, db = gradient(w, b)
        if (dw ** 2 + db ** 2) ** 0.5 < tol:   # ground is flat: stop
            return step, w, b
        w, b = w - lr * dw, b - lr * db
        if loss(w, b) > 1e9:
            return "diverged at step", step + 1, None
    return max_steps, w, b

w, b = 3.0, 0.0
for step in range(1, 3):
    dw, db = gradient(w, b)
    w, b = w - 0.01 * dw, b - 0.01 * db
    print(step, round(dw, 4), round(db, 4), round(w, 4), round(b, 4), round(loss(w, b), 4))

steps, w, b = descend(0.01)
print(steps, "steps: w =", round(w, 4), "b =", round(b, 4), "loss =", round(loss(w, b), 4))
print(descend(0.014))

# how many steps until (w, b) rounds to Lesson 03's grid answer, 2.15 and 5.6?
w, b, steps = 3.0, 0.0, 0
while not (round(w, 2) == 2.15 and round(b, 1) == 5.6):
    dw, db = gradient(w, b)
    w, b, steps = w - 0.01 * dw, b - 0.01 * db, steps + 1
print(steps, "steps, versus 24,381 grid settings")

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"A bigger learning rate always trains faster."Only up to a point. On our bowl, η = 0.005 lands in five steps, but η = 0.015 (just three times bigger) overshoots further every step and the loss passes 1,000 by step ten. If your loss goes up or turns into nan, lower the learning rate first.
"The gradient points at the best setting."It points down the steepest slope where you are, which is often not toward the bottom. From ŷ = 3x the first step pushed b from 0 to −0.024, away from its best value 5.6. Gradient descent gets there by re-measuring every step, not by aiming.
"Gradient descent always finds the best possible model."For a single bowl like ours, yes, given a sensible η. For bumpy losses like neural networks it finds a low point near where it started, and even then it stops when the ground is nearly flat, not exactly at the bottom: our run stopped at 2.1505 and 5.5955, close to 2.15 and 5.6.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. With b held at 5.6, the slope is dL/dw = 144w − 309.6. You're at w = 2 and η = 0.005. Which way does w move, and where does it land?Show answer

Slope: 144 × 2 − 309.6 = −21.6, negative, so downhill is to the right and w goes up. New w = 2 − 0.005 × (−21.6) = 2 + 0.108 = 2.108. The bottom is at 2.15, so the gap shrank from 0.15 to 0.042, the same × 0.28 as in step 4.

2. You start a training run and print the loss every step. It goes up every few steps, swings more and more, and then prints nan. What's the most likely cause, and the first fix?Show answer

The learning rate is too big: every step overshoots the valley and lands higher up the other side, like η = 0.015 on our bowl, until the numbers overflow. Cut the learning rate, for example by 10×, and run again.

3. At some setting, ∂L/∂w = 0.0004 and ∂L/∂b = −0.0007. With the stopping rule "gradient length under 0.001", do you stop?Show answer

Length: √(0.0004² + 0.0007²) = √0.00000065 ≈ 0.00081, which is under 0.001, so yes, stop. The ground is nearly flat in both directions, so more steps would barely change the knobs.

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

Gradient descent trains a model by measuring the slope of the loss for every knob and moving each knob a small step against it (learning rate × slope), over and over, until the ground goes flat.
