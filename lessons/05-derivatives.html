05 · Derivatives and slopes, intuitively

A derivative answers one question: if I nudge this input a tiny bit, how much does the output move, and in which direction? For a model, the input is a knob and the output is its error, so the answer tells you which way to turn every knob without trying them all.

a · Where we areFrom trying everything to feeling the slope

In Lesson 03 we fitted a line to five flats by brute force: 24,381 knob settings, each scored, best one kept. Lesson 04 packed the predictions into one line, ŷ = Xw + b. What we still lack is a smart way to choose w and b. Today's tool is the derivative: from where you stand, it tells you which way is downhill and how steep it is, using only nearby information.

b · Why this mattersEvery training step is a pile of slopes

Grid search dies quickly. Ten values for each of a million knobs is 101,000,000 settings, more than any computer could ever try. Real models don't search at all. On every training step they compute, for each parameter, the slope of the error with respect to that parameter, then nudge every parameter a little in the downhill direction. When a PyTorch script calls loss.backward(), that is what happens: one slope is computed and stored for every weight in the model. A model with billions of parameters gets billions of slopes per step. This lesson is about what one of those numbers means.

c · The intuitionWalking down a hill in thick fog

You are on a hillside in fog so dense you can see only your own boots. You want to reach the bottom of the valley. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the ground: if it tilts down to your left, go left. If it is steep, you are probably far from the bottom. If it is perfectly flat, you might be there.

  • Your position is the knob (a weight like w).
  • Your height is the error the model makes at that knob setting.
  • The tilt under your feet is the derivative: how much height changes per step, and which way.

A car's speedometer is the same idea in another costume. Average speed is total distance over total time: 120 km in 2 hours is 60 km/h. But the speedometer shows speed right now, which is the distance covered in a tiny slice of time divided by that tiny slice. "Rate of change at one instant" is exactly what a derivative is.

d · How it actually worksSix steps, from a straight line to two knobs

1. The slope of a straight line

Slope is rise over run: how much the output goes up when the input goes up by 1. Lesson 03's fitted line was ŷ = 2.15x + 5.6. Its slope is 2.15: every extra 100 sq ft (x up by 1) adds ₹2,150 of rent (ŷ up by 2.15). On a straight line the slope is the same everywhere, so one number describes the whole line.

2. Curves: the average slope between two points

On a curve the steepness keeps changing. Take f(x) = x². Between x = 3 and x = 4 the output goes from 9 to 16: a rise of 7 over a run of 1, so an average slope of 7. Write the gap between the two inputs as h:

average slope = (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h
Average rate of change
The rise f(x + h) − f(x) divided by the run h. It is the slope of the straight line through the two points on the curve.
Secant line
The name for that straight line through two points of a curve. It is an honest summary of the stretch between them, but it blurs over any bends in between.

The slopes close in on 6. Algebra shows why. (x + h)² expands to x² + 2xh + h², so the rise is 2xh + h², and dividing by h leaves

slope over a gap h = 2x + h

The + h is the error from using a gap at all. As h shrinks it vanishes, leaving 2x. At x = 3 that is 6. The number the slopes settle on is the instantaneous slope, and the straight line that just touches the curve at that point with that slope is the tangent line.

4. The derivative, named

f′(x) = the value of (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h as h shrinks toward 0
Derivative
The instantaneous slope of f at the point x. Books write the "as h shrinks toward 0" part as limh→0, read "the limit as h goes to zero": the value the expression heads toward as h gets as small as you like, without ever being exactly 0 (dividing by 0 is meaningless).
f′(x), read "f prime of x"
One way to write the derivative. Note it is itself a function: for f(x) = x², f′(x) = 2x, a different slope at every point.
df/dx, read "d f by d x"
The same thing in a different notation. Read d as "a tiny change in", so df/dx means "tiny change in f divided by the tiny change in x that caused it". It is handy because it names which input you nudged. You'll see dL/dw a lot: how the loss L responds to the weight w.

Reading a derivative needs only its sign and size. Positive: the output rises as the input increases, so to go down, decrease the input. Negative: the output falls as the input increases, so increase it. Zero: the ground is flat right here, as at the bottom of a valley. Large in size: steep, a small nudge moves the output a lot.

6. A real loss with one knob

Take flat C from Lesson 03: 800 sq ft (x = 8) renting for ₹22,000 (y = 22). Keep the model as simple as possible, ŷ = w·x, one knob, no bias. The loss is the number we want small; here, the squared miss:

L(w) = (w·x − y)² = (8w − 22)²
Symbols
w is the knob (₹ thousands per 100 sq ft). x = 8 and y = 22 are fixed facts about the flat. w·x is the guess, w·x − y is the miss, and L(w) is the miss squared. Lesson 07 covers why we square it.

Numeric version first. At w = 2 the guess is 16, the miss is −6, and L = 36. Nudge to w = 2.001: the guess is 16.008, the miss −5.992, and L = 35.904064. The loss changed by −0.095936 over a nudge of 0.001, so the slope is about −95.936.

Now with the rules. Multiply out the square: (8w − 22)² = 64w² − 352w + 484. Rule by rule: 64w² → 128w, −352w → −352, 484 → 0. So

L′(w) = 128w − 352, and L′(2) = 256 − 352 = −96

The numeric guess, −95.936, was off only by a little bit of leftover gap, just like the + h earlier. The sign is negative, so increasing w lowers the loss. The slope is zero where 128w = 352, that is w = 2.75, which is exactly 22 / 8: the knob at which the guess equals the real rent. We found the bottom of the valley without searching.

7. Two knobs: partial derivatives

Put the bias back: L(w, b) = (w·x + b − y)². Now there are two knobs, and the trick is simple: nudge one knob, hold the other still. The slope you get is called a partial derivative, written with a curly d: ∂L/∂w ("partial L by partial w") and ∂L/∂b.

Stand at w = 2, b = 4. The guess is 16 + 4 = 20, the miss −2, and L = 4.

  • Nudge w, hold b at 4: L(2.001, 4) = (−1.992)² = 3.968064, a change of −0.031936 over 0.001, so ∂L/∂w ≈ −31.936. Exactly: with b = 4 fixed the loss is (8w − 18)² = 64w² − 288w + 324, whose derivative 128w − 288 is −32 at w = 2.
  • Nudge b, hold w at 2: L(2, 4.001) = (−1.999)² = 3.996001, so ∂L/∂b ≈ −3.999. Exactly: with w = 2 fixed the loss is (b − 6)² = b² − 12b + 36, whose derivative 2b − 12 is −4 at b = 4.

Both are negative, so both knobs should go up, and the loss is eight times more sensitive to w than to b right here. Collect the partials into a list, [−32, −4], and you have a vector (Lesson 04) called the gradient. It points uphill; its opposite points downhill. That is the whole idea behind gradient descent in Lesson 08.

Compare the cost. Brute force scored 24,381 settings. The nudging method needs one extra loss evaluation per knob, and the exact slopes need only a formula. Training libraries use a smarter bookkeeping method (backpropagation, later in the course) that gets every partial derivative at once for roughly the cost of a couple of passes through the model, whether it has two knobs or two billion.

f · Worked exampleBy hand, then in Python

The loss for flat C, L(w) = (8w − 22)², at w = 2:

  • L(2) = (16 − 22)² = (−6)² = 36
  • L(2.001) = (16.008 − 22)² = (−5.992)² = 35.904064
  • Numeric slope: (35.904064 − 36) / 0.001 = −95.936
  • Exact slope from the rules: L′(w) = 128w − 352, so L′(2) = −96

Following the slope. The slope says "go up, steeply". A sensible rule is to move against the slope, scaled down by a small number called the step size (here 0.005; Lesson 08 is about choosing it): w ← w − 0.005 × L′(w). The first step is 2 − 0.005 × (−96) = 2.48.

Five slope calculations brought the loss from 36 to about 0.001, closing in on w = 2.75. The same code, in plain Python:

# one flat (flat C from Lesson 03): 800 sq ft -> x = 8, rent 22k -> y = 22
x, y = 8, 22

def loss(w, b=0):
    """squared miss for this one flat"""
    return (w * x + b - y) ** 2

def slope(f, a, h):
    """average rate of change of f between a and a + h"""
    return (f(a + h) - f(a)) / h

# 1. shrink the gap on f(x) = x^2 at x = 3 (exact answer 2x = 6)
sq = lambda t: t ** 2
for h in [1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    print(h, round(slope(sq, 3, h), 6))
# 1 7.0
# 0.1 6.1
# 0.01 6.01
# 0.001 6.001

# 2. the loss for one knob, L(w) = (8w - 22)^2, at w = 2
print(loss(2))                                # 36
print(round(slope(loss, 2, 0.001), 3))        # -95.936   numeric slope
print(128 * 2 - 352)                          # -96       exact: L'(w) = 128w - 352

# 3. two knobs, w = 2 and b = 4: nudge one, hold the other
w, b, h = 2, 4, 0.001
print(loss(w, b))                                       # 4
print(round((loss(w + h, b) - loss(w, b)) / h, 3))      # -31.936   dL/dw, numeric
print(round((loss(w, b + h) - loss(w, b)) / h, 3))      # -3.999    dL/db, numeric
print(128 * w - 288, 2 * b - 12)                        # -32 -4    exact, from the rules

# 4. follow the slope downhill: w <- w - 0.005 * slope
w = 2
for step in range(5):
    w = w - 0.005 * (128 * w - 352)
    print(step + 1, round(w, 4), round(loss(w), 4))
# 1 2.48 4.6656
# 2 2.6528 0.6047
# 3 2.715 0.0784
# 4 2.7374 0.0102
# 5 2.7455 0.0013

The numeric slopes are always a hair off the exact ones, by an amount that shrinks with h. In practice libraries use exact derivatives (worked out automatically), and engineers use the nudge method as a sanity check, called a gradient check, when they suspect a bug.

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"The derivative of a function is one number."It is a new function: one slope for every point. For x² it is 2x, which is −4 at x = −2, 0 at x = 0 and 6 at x = 3. "The derivative is −96" is shorthand for "at this particular w".
"The slope tells you how far away the bottom is."It tells you direction and local steepness only. At w = 2 the slope is −96, yet the bottom is just 0.75 away. Step by the full −96 and you would fly far past it. That is why we multiply by a small step size and recompute the slope after every step.
"Slope zero means you found the best setting."It means the ground is flat here. That could be the bottom of a valley, but also a hilltop or a flat shelf. Our one-flat loss is a simple bowl, so its only flat point is the bottom. Neural network losses are bumpier, which Lesson 08 and later lessons deal with.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. Use the power rule to find the derivative of f(x) = x³ at x = 2. Then check it numerically with h = 0.01.

Show answer

f′(x) = 3x², so f′(2) = 12. Numerically: 2.01³ = 8.120601, so (8.120601 − 8) / 0.01 = 12.0601, close to 12, off by a little leftover gap.

2. A flat has x = 4, y = 12, so L(w) = (4w − 12)² = 16w² − 96w + 144. What is L′(1), which way should w move, and where is the slope zero?

Show answer

L′(w) = 32w − 96, so L′(1) = −64. Negative means increasing w lowers the loss, so move w up. The slope is zero at w = 3, where the guess 4 × 3 = 12 matches the rent exactly.

3. At some setting, ∂L/∂w = 10 and ∂L/∂b = −2. Which way should each knob move, and which one matters more right now?

Show answer

Move against each slope: w down (its slope is positive), b up (its slope is negative). The loss is five times more sensitive to w here, so a nudge to w changes it more. The gradient is [10, −2]; downhill is [−10, 2].

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

A derivative is the slope at a single point: how much the output moves per tiny nudge of the input, and its sign tells you which way is downhill.
