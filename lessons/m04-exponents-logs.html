M04 · Exponents and logarithms

An exponent counts how many times you multiply. A logarithm runs that backwards: it tells you the count. With those two ideas, plus one special number called e, you can read the loss that trains every classifier.

a · Where this is usedThe AI lessons that need it

Exponents and e come back in Lesson 10 · Logistic regression (coming), Lesson 21 · Activation functions (coming), Lesson 31 · Tokenization (coming) and Lesson 40 · Decoding (coming). The probabilities we take logs of are the ones from Lesson 06 · Probability basics for ML, like the spam filter's "0.92".

b · Why this mattersThe model said 0.92. How much should that cost?

In Lesson 06 a spam filter said "spam with probability 0.92". If the email really is spam, that answer was good, and its cost should be small. If the filter had said 0.08, it was confidently wrong, and the cost should be large. The usual cost is −ln p, where p is the probability the model gave the right answer: 0.0834 for the good answer, 2.5257 for the bad one, about 30 times more. To read that formula you need logarithms, and to understand logarithms you need exponents. That's this page.

c · The intuitionFolding paper

Take a sheet of paper and fold it in half. Now it's 2 layers thick. Fold again: 4 layers. Again: 8. Each fold doubles the thickness. Two questions come up naturally:

  • Forwards: "I folded 3 times. How many layers?" You double three times: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. That is an exponent.
  • Backwards: "I see 8 layers. How many folds did it take?" You count the doublings: 3. That is a logarithm.

Now unfold it. Each unfold halves the layers: 8, 4, 2, 1. If you could keep going past 1, you'd get half a layer, then a quarter. That's where negative exponents live. Logarithms are just counting folds, and everything below is a careful version of this one picture.

d · How it actually worksEight steps, from 2 × 2 × 2 to −ln p

1. An exponent is repeated multiplication

Writing 2 × 2 × 2 gets tiring, so we write 23, read "2 to the power 3" or "2 cubed". It means: start from 1, multiply by 2 three times.

23 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8
102 = 10 × 10 = 100
51 = 5
Base, written a
The number that gets multiplied. In 23 the base is 2.
Exponent (or power), written n
The small raised number: how many copies of the base are multiplied. In 23 it is 3. In code it is written 2 ** 3 in Python and Math.pow(2, 3) or 2 ** 3 in JavaScript.
an
"a to the n": n copies of a, multiplied together. The result grows very fast: 210 = 1,024, 220 = 1,048,576.

2. The rules come from counting factors

You don't need to memorise the exponent rules. Write the copies out and count them. A factor is one of the things being multiplied.

RuleWhy (count the factors), with an example
am · an = am+nm copies, then n more copies: m + n copies.
23 · 24 = 8 × 16 = 128 = 27

25 / 22 = 32 / 4 = 8 = 23

(22)3 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 = 26

The first rule is the important one for us: multiplying powers of the same base adds their exponents. Hold on to it; logarithms are built on it.

3. Zero and negative exponents: keep halving

"Multiply 2 by itself zero times" or "minus one times" sounds like nonsense. But look at the pattern as the exponent goes down by 1: each step divides by 2 (one fewer copy of 2).

Keep the pattern going and the answers are forced: 20 = 1 and 2−n = 1 / 2n. The division rule agrees: 23 / 23 = 23−3 = 20, and anything divided by itself is 1. This is the paper unfolding past one layer. One fact matters a lot later: a positive base raised to any power is still positive. Negative exponents make it small, never negative or zero.

4. The logarithm: how many times do I multiply?

The question "2 to the what is 8?" has the answer 3. We give that question a name:

log2 8 = 3 because 23 = 8
logb x, read "log base b of x"
The exponent you need on b to get x. In words: how many times do I multiply by b, starting from 1, to reach x? So logb x = y means exactly the same thing as by = x.
Inverse
Two operations that undo each other, like "add 5" and "subtract 5". "Raise 2 to a power" and "take log base 2" are inverses: log2(23) = 3 and 2log2 8 = 8.

A few by counting: log2 1024 = 10 (ten doublings), log10 1000 = 3 (1000 has three tens multiplied together), log2 1 = 0 (no steps at all). And from the halving table, log2 0.125 = −3. That last one is worth stating as a rule, because probabilities live there: the log of a number between 0 and 1 is negative, and it gets more negative the closer the number is to 0. You can't take the log of 0 or of a negative number: no power of a positive base ever lands there.

5. Logs turn multiplication into addition

Step 2 said multiplying powers adds exponents. A log is an exponent, so:

log(x · y) = log x + log y

Check with base 2: 8 × 16 = 128. Counting steps: log2 8 = 3, log2 16 = 4, and log2 128 = 7 = 3 + 4. Three doublings followed by four more is seven doublings. This works for any base, and it's the main reason logs show up in machine learning.

Why it helps: computers can't hold tiny products. A language model gives each word of a sentence a probability, and the chance of the whole sentence is all of those multiplied together (if we treat them as independent, Lesson 06's AND rule). Say each of 200 words gets probability 0.02. The product is 0.02200, about 10−340. A standard 64-bit float can't store anything smaller than about 5 × 10−324, so the product quietly becomes 0. That's called underflow, and once you hit 0 you can't compare two sentences any more. Adding logs instead gives 200 × ln 0.02 = −782.4, an ordinary number. So models almost never multiply probabilities; they add their logs.

6. The special number e, from compound interest

Put $1 in a bank that pays 100% interest a year. Paid once at the end of the year, you get $2. Now suppose the bank splits it into 2 half-year payments of 50% each, and the second payment earns interest on the first: 1 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25. Split into 12 monthly payments of 1/12 each: (1 + 1/12)12 = 2.613035. In general, with n payments:

More slices help, but less and less. The total doesn't grow forever; it creeps towards a fixed number, e ≈ 2.718282. Jacob Bernoulli ran into this limit in 1683 while studying exactly this interest question. e is "what 100% growth becomes when it is compounding all the time", and that is why it keeps showing up wherever something grows or shrinks in proportion to its own size. 7. ln and exp: the log and power for base e

exp(x), also written ex
e raised to the power x. exp(0) = 1, exp(1) = e ≈ 2.718. Like every positive base, it is always positive, whatever x is.
ln x, the natural logarithm
Log base e: ln x = loge x. It undoes exp: ln(ex) = x and eln x = x. So ln e = 1 and ln 1 = 0. In Python, math.log(x) with one argument is ln, and math.exp(x) is ex.

Why base e and not 2 or 10? Any base would work: switching base only multiplies every log by a fixed number (ln x ≈ 0.693 × log2 x). Base e makes the slopes from Lesson 05 come out the cleanest (the slope of ex is ex itself), so ML uses it by default. When an ML paper writes "log", it almost always means ln.

Two previews. In Lesson 10 a classifier turns a score z into a probability with the sigmoid, 1 / (1 + e−z). A score of 0 gives 1 / (1 + 1) = 0.5; a score of 2.4423 gives e−2.4423 ≈ 0.087 and 1 / 1.087 ≈ 0.92, our spam filter's answer. And because ex is never negative, Lessons 31 and 40 use it (in softmax) to turn any list of scores into positive numbers that can be scaled into probabilities.

8. The shape of −ln p: a preview of log loss

Now put it together. Let p be the probability a model gave to the answer that turned out to be right. p is between 0 and 1, so ln p is 0 or negative (step 4). Put a minus sign in front and you get a cost that is 0 or positive:

cost = −ln p
p given to the right answer−ln pwhat happened
10certain and right: no cost
0.920.0834confident and right: tiny cost
0.50.6931a coin toss: medium cost
0.082.5257confident and wrong: big cost
0.014.6052very confident and wrong: bigger still

Two things to notice. Near p = 1 the curve is almost flat: going from 0.92 to 1 saves only 0.08. Near p = 0 it shoots up without limit, because ln p heads to minus infinity. So being confidently wrong is punished hard, much harder than being unsure. That is exactly the behaviour Lesson 07 wants from a loss. And the "logs add" rule means the cost of a whole batch of answers is just the sum of their −ln p's.

f · Worked exampleBy hand, then in Python

Exponents. 23 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. 20 = 1. 2−1 = 1/2 = 0.5, 2−3 = 1/8 = 0.125. Counting factors: 23 · 24 = 8 × 16 = 128 = 27.

Logs. log2 8 = 3, log2 1024 = 10, log2 0.125 = −3. Multiplication into addition: log2(8 × 16) = log2 128 = 7 = 3 + 4.

e. n = 1: 2. n = 2: 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25. n = 12: 1.083333…12 = 2.613035. Then 2.714567 for daily, 2.718280 for a million, closing in on e = 2.718281828…. Also ln e = 1, ln 1 = 0, and eln 5 = 5 (exp undoes ln).

Sigmoid preview. e−2.4423 ≈ 0.087, so 1 / (1 + 0.087) ≈ 0.92.

Costs. −ln 0.92 = 0.0834, −ln 0.5 = 0.6931, −ln 0.08 = 2.5257, −ln 0.01 = 4.6052. Logs add, so two answers of 0.92 and 0.5 cost 0.0834 + 0.6931 = 0.7765, the same as −ln(0.92 × 0.5) = −ln 0.46 = 0.7765.

Underflow. 200 words at 0.02 each: the product is about 10−340, below the smallest float, so it becomes 0. The sum of logs is 200 × ln 0.02 = 200 × (−3.912) = −782.4.

import math

# exponents: repeated multiplication
print(2 ** 3, 2 ** 0, 2 ** -1, 2 ** -3)    # 8 1 0.5 0.125
print(2 ** 3 * 2 ** 4, 2 ** 7)              # 128 128

# logarithms: "how many times do I multiply?"
print(math.log2(8), math.log2(1024), math.log2(0.125))   # 3.0 10.0 -3.0
print(math.log2(8 * 16), math.log2(8) + math.log2(16))   # 7.0 7.0

# e from compound growth: $1 at 100% a year, paid in n slices
for n in [1, 2, 12, 365, 1_000_000]:
    print(n, round((1 + 1 / n) ** n, 6))
# 1 2.0
# 2 2.25
# 12 2.613035
# 365 2.714567
# 1000000 2.71828
print(math.e, math.exp(1))       # 2.718281828459045 2.718281828459045
print(math.log(math.e), math.log(1), round(math.exp(math.log(5)), 10))   # 1.0 0.0 5.0

# the sigmoid from Lesson 10 uses e: a score of 2.4423 becomes 0.92
print(round(1 / (1 + math.exp(-2.4423)), 4))   # 0.92

# -ln p: the cost of giving the right answer probability p
for p in [0.92, 0.5, 0.08, 0.01]:
    print(p, round(-math.log(p), 4))
# 0.92 0.0834
# 0.5 0.6931
# 0.08 2.5257
# 0.01 4.6052

# multiplying many small probabilities underflows; adding logs does not
probs = [0.02] * 200
product = 1.0
for p in probs:
    product *= p
print(product)                                          # 0.0
print(round(sum(math.log(p) for p in probs), 2))        # -782.4

Python prints the million-slice value as 2.71828 because rounding to 6 places gives 2.718280 and the trailing zero is dropped. The underflow line is the one to remember: a plain loop of multiplications returns exactly 0.0, with no error or warning.

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"2³ means 2 × 3."It means three 2s multiplied: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, not 6. The exponent counts copies; it is not a second number to multiply by. The gap grows fast: 210 is 1,024, while 2 × 10 is 20.
"A negative exponent gives a negative number."It gives a small positive number: 2−3 = 1/8 = 0.125. The minus sign means "divide instead of multiply". It's the log of a number below 1 that is negative, which is why log loss needs a minus sign in front to become a positive cost.
"log(x + y) = log x + log y."Logs turn multiplication into addition, not addition into addition. log2(8 + 8) = log2 16 = 4, but log2 8 + log2 8 = 6, which is log2(8 × 8) = log2 64. There is no simple rule for the log of a sum.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. What is 10−2, and what is log10 0.01?

Show answer

10−2 = 1 / 102 = 1/100 = 0.01. The log asks the same question backwards, "10 to the what is 0.01?", so log10 0.01 = −2. A number below 1 has a negative log.

2. A model gave the right answer probability 0.5 on one email and 0.25 on another. Using −ln 0.5 = 0.6931, what is −ln 0.25?

Show answer

0.25 = 0.5 × 0.5, and logs turn multiplication into addition, so −ln 0.25 = 0.6931 + 0.6931 = 1.3863. Halving the probability given to the right answer always adds the same 0.6931 to the cost.

3. Why do models add up log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?

Show answer

Multiplying hundreds of numbers below 1 produces a value too small for a float to store, so it rounds to 0 (underflow) and every long sentence looks equally impossible. Because ln(x · y) = ln x + ln y, adding logs carries the same information as the product, but as an ordinary number like −782.4.

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

An exponent counts how many times you multiply, a log reads that count back, and because logs turn products into sums, −ln p gives a cost that is tiny when a model is confidently right and huge when it is confidently wrong.
