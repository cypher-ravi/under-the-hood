M02 · Squares, square roots and absolute value

Squaring a number means drawing a square with that side and measuring its area. That one picture explains why negative misses turn positive, why big misses get punished so hard, what a square root undoes, and how Pythagoras measures the distance between any two points, even in 768 dimensions.

  • Lesson 03 · A model is a function: scoring a line by its total squared error.
  • Lesson 04 · Vectors and matrices: a vector's length, √(x₁² + x₂² + …).
  • Lesson 05 · Derivatives: the bowl-shaped loss x².
  • Lesson 07 · Loss functions (coming): squared error versus absolute error. Lesson 13 · Regularization (coming) and Lesson 17 · k-nearest neighbours (coming): sizes of weights and distances between examples.

b · Why this mattersMisses have a sign, but "how wrong" shouldn't

When Lesson 03's line guessed flat A's rent ₹2,000 too low and flat E's ₹5,000 too high, the misses were −2 and +5. Add raw misses and the lows cancel the highs, so a terrible model can score zero. Every loss in ML first has to make each miss positive, and the two standard ways to do it, squaring and absolute value, are what this page is about. The square root then comes back as the way to turn "sum of squares" into a distance, which is how k-nearest neighbours decides which examples are "close".

c · The intuitionTiles on a floor, and steps from your door

Squares are floor tiles. A square room 3 m on each side needs 9 one-metre tiles: 3 rows of 3. Double the side to 6 m and you need 36 tiles, four times as many. The tile count is the side times itself. That count is what "3 squared" means. A room can't have a negative size: if you paced out the side walking backwards, the floor still needs 9 tiles.

A square root is reading the room backwards. The shop says "this square room has 25 tiles". How long is a wall? You look for the side that, times itself, makes 25: it's 5.

Absolute value is "how many steps from your door". Your friend is 3 doors to the left, another friend is 3 doors to the right. Different directions, same distance: 3. Absolute value keeps the "how far" and throws away the "which way".

Both squaring and absolute value throw away direction. The difference is in how they treat big numbers: absolute value counts steps (2 steps then 4 steps), squaring counts tiles (4 tiles then 16 tiles). That difference is the whole story of squared versus absolute error.

d · How it actually worksSix steps, from one square to the distance between points

1. Squaring is a number times itself

x², read "x squared"
The number x multiplied by itself: x² = x × x. The small raised 2 is called an exponent; it counts how many copies of x get multiplied. So 3² = 3 × 3 = 9 and 1.5² = 2.25. In Python it is written x ** 2.
Why "squared"
Because x² is the area of a square whose side is x: x rows of x unit tiles.

What about negatives? (−3)² = (−3) × (−3) = 9, the same as 3². If "minus times minus is plus" was only ever a rule to memorise, here is why it has to be true. Watch the pattern as you multiply −3 by smaller and smaller numbers:

−3 ×3210−1−2−3
=−9−6−30369

Every step to the right lowers the second number by 1, so the answer goes up by 3. Nothing stops at zero, so the pattern carries on: 3, 6, 9. Any other answer would break the steady climb. So every square is zero or positive, and x² and (−x)² are always equal. Squaring erases the sign.

The same fact as a picture: multiplying by −3 means "stretch by 3, then turn around". Square a negative and you turn around twice, which faces you forward again.

One trap: −3² written without brackets usually means −(3²) = −9. Python agrees: -3 ** 2 is -9, while (-3) ** 2 is 9. When you square a miss, bracket it.

2. Squares grow fast

Count the tiles as the side grows by one each time:

Double the side and the area goes up four times: 2² = 4 but 4² = 16; 5² = 25 but 10² = 100. The doubled square has twice as many rows, and each row is twice as long, so it holds 2 × 2 = 4 times the tiles. Ten times the side gives a hundred times the area. In a loss this means one big miss costs more than several small ones that add up to the same size. A single miss of 4 costs 16; two misses of 2 cost only 4 + 4 = 8.

This is also where the "bowl" in Lesson 05 comes from. That lesson's loss for flat C, L(w) = (8w − 22)², is a miss squared. It can never go below 0, it is exactly 0 when the miss is 0 (at w = 2.75), and it rises faster and faster on both sides. Draw it and you get a bowl.

3. The square root undoes the square

√a, read "the square root of a"
The side of a square whose area is a: the number that, times itself, gives a. √25 = 5 because 5 × 5 = 25. √9 = 3, √1 = 1, √0 = 0. In Python: math.sqrt(a), or a ** 0.5.
Only the non-negative side
Both 3 and −3 square to 9, but a side length is never negative, so √9 means 3 by agreement. And √(−9) has no answer among ordinary numbers, because no ordinary number squares to something negative. In this course we only take roots of things that are already zero or positive (sums of squares), so it never comes up.

Most roots are not whole numbers. √2 is the side of a square with area 2. It must be between 1 (area 1) and 2 (area 4); closer search gives 1.414 × 1.414 = 1.999396, so √2 ≈ 1.414. A calculator does that same narrowing-down, just very fast.

Square and root cancel: √(5²) = √25 = 5. But start with a negative and see what happens: √((−3)²) = √9 = 3. The square erased the sign, and the root cannot bring it back. So for any number, √(x²) is its size with the sign dropped, which is exactly the next idea.

4. Absolute value: distance from zero

|x|, read "the absolute value of x"
How far x is from 0 on the number line, ignoring which side. |−3| = 3, |3| = 3, |0| = 0. The rule: if x is negative, flip its sign; otherwise leave it. In Python: abs(x).
The same bars on a vector
Lesson 04 writes a vector's length as |x| too. It is the same idea, "size, ignoring direction", stretched to arrows. Step 6 shows how to compute it.

5. Two ways to make misses positive

miss
Guess minus truth, ŷ − y, as in Lesson 03. Positive means "guessed too high", negative means "too low".
Σ, the capital Greek letter sigma
"Add this up over every example." Σ miss² means square each miss, then add the squares. Math M03 covers Σ properly.

Take Lesson 03's first guess, ŷ = 3x, on the five flats. Its misses are −2, −1, +2, +2, +5.

The plain total, 6, hides how bad things are: the three too-high misses (+9 together) are partly cancelled by the two too-low ones (−3). Both fixes stop the cancelling:

  • Total absolute error, Σ |miss| = 12. Every unit of miss costs the same. Flat E's miss of 5 is 5 of the 12, about 42%.
  • Total squared error, Σ miss² = 38, Lesson 03's score. Flat E alone is 25 of the 38, about 66%. Squaring makes the worst flat dominate, so a model trained on it will work hardest to fix its biggest misses.

Lesson 03's better guess, ŷ = 2x + 6, has misses 0, −1, 0, −2, −1: total absolute error 4, total squared error 6. Both scores agree it is better. They do not always agree on how much better, and when the data has a few wild outliers they can even pick different winners. Squared error also has a smooth bottom (the slope of x² is 2x, Lesson 05), while |x| has a sharp corner at 0. Lesson 07 weighs these trade-offs.

6. Pythagoras: distance between two points

In a right-angled triangle (one corner is a perfect "L"), call the two short sides a and b and the long slanted side c. Pythagoras' theorem says:

a² + b² = c² so c = √(a² + b²)

With a = 4 and b = 3: 16 + 9 = 25, so c = √25 = 5. In squares-as-areas language: the square on the long side has exactly as much floor as the two squares on the short sides together. The figure in section e shows why.

From triangle to distance. Two points on a grid, p = (3, 1) and q = (1, 2), the arrows u and v from Lesson 04. Walk from one to the other using only "across" and "up" moves and you have drawn a right-angled triangle:

  • across gap: dx = 1 − 3 = −2; up gap: dy = 2 − 1 = 1
  • square them (signs vanish, so it doesn't matter which point you start from): 4 and 1
  • add and take the root: √(4 + 1) = √5 ≈ 2.236
distance(p, q) = √(dx² + dy²)

The length of a vector is just its distance from the origin (0, 0), so the gaps are the entries themselves. Lesson 04's u + v = [4, 3] has length √(16 + 9) = 5, and u = [3, 1] has length √(9 + 1) = √10 ≈ 3.162, the numbers that lesson uses.

More dimensions, same rule. For three entries, add a third square: |[1, 2, 2]| = √(1 + 4 + 4) = √9 = 3. For a 768-entry embedding you add 768 squares. You can't draw it, but the arithmetic never changes. This "square the gaps, add, root" distance is called the Euclidean distance, and it is how k-nearest neighbours (Lesson 17) finds the training examples most like a new one.

f · Worked exampleBy hand, then in Python

Errors for Lesson 03's guess 1. Misses −2, −1, +2, +2, +5.

  • Plain sum: −2 − 1 + 2 + 2 + 5 = 6 (signs cancel, misleading)
  • Absolute values: 2, 1, 2, 2, 5, total 12
  • Squares: 4, 1, 4, 4, 25, total 38
  • Guess 2 (misses 0, −1, 0, −2, −1): absolute total 0 + 1 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 4, squared total 0 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 1 = 6
  • Spread out (2, −2, 2, −2, 2) versus bunched up (0, 0, 0, 4, 6): absolute totals 10 and 10; squared totals 5 × 4 = 20 and 16 + 36 = 52

Roots. √25 = 5; √((−3)²) = √9 = 3 = |−3|; √2 ≈ 1.414.

Distances. From (3, 1) to (1, 2): √((−2)² + 1²) = √5 ≈ 2.236. Length of [4, 3]: √(16 + 9) = 5. Length of [3, 1]: √10 ≈ 3.162. Length of [1, 2, 2]: √9 = 3.

import math

# Lesson 03, guess 1 (y-hat = 3x): miss = guess - real rent, for flats A to E
misses = [-2, -1, 2, 2, 5]

print([m ** 2 for m in misses])        # each miss squared
print([abs(m) for m in misses])        # each miss's size, sign dropped
print(sum(misses))                     # plain sum: signs cancel
print(sum(abs(m) for m in misses))     # total absolute error
print(sum(m ** 2 for m in misses))     # total squared error

# Lesson 03, guess 2 (y-hat = 2x + 6)
guess2 = [0, -1, 0, -2, -1]
print(sum(abs(m) for m in guess2), sum(m ** 2 for m in guess2))

# same total size, spread out vs bunched up
spread, bunched = [2, -2, 2, -2, 2], [0, 0, 0, 4, 6]
print(sum(abs(m) for m in spread), sum(abs(m) for m in bunched))
print(sum(m ** 2 for m in spread), sum(m ** 2 for m in bunched))

# square root undoes the square, and sqrt(x squared) is |x|
print(math.sqrt(25), math.sqrt((-3) ** 2), round(math.sqrt(2), 3))

def distance(p, q):
    """Pythagoras: square each gap, add them, take the square root"""
    return math.sqrt(sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(p, q)))

print(round(distance([3, 1], [1, 2]), 3))   # u to v from Lesson 04
print(distance([0, 0], [4, 3]))             # length of u + v = [4, 3]
print(round(distance([0, 0], [3, 1]), 3))   # length of u
print(distance([0, 0, 0], [1, 2, 2]))       # three entries: same rule

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"√(a² + b²) = a + b."A root does not split across a plus. √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, but √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7. Walking 4 across and 3 up is 7 steps along the grid, but the straight line is only 5. The shortcut across the diagonal is the whole point of Pythagoras.
"Squared error is just absolute error made bigger."It changes the ranking, not just the scale. Spread-out misses (2, −2, 2, −2, 2) and bunched-up ones (0, 0, 0, 4, 6) tie on absolute error at 10, but squared error scores them 20 and 52. With one wild outlier in the data, a squared-error model will bend a lot to reduce it; an absolute-error model barely cares.
"Squaring always makes a number bigger."Only for sizes above 1. 0.5² = 0.25 and 0.1² = 0.01: small misses shrink when squared. That is one more reason squared error barely notices tiny misses and fixates on large ones.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. A model's misses on four houses are +3, −3, +1, −1. What are the plain sum, the total absolute error and the total squared error?

Show answer

Plain sum: 3 − 3 + 1 − 1 = 0, which wrongly says "perfect". Absolute: 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8. Squared: 9 + 9 + 1 + 1 = 20. Set the misses lab to these values (fifth slider at 0) to check.

2. Flat E's miss shrinks from 5 to 4. By how much does the total squared error of Lesson 03's guess 1 drop? And the total absolute error?

Show answer

Squared: 25 → 16, so it drops by 9, from 38 to 29. Absolute: 5 → 4, a drop of 1, from 12 to 11. The same one-unit fix is worth nine times more under squared error, because the miss was big.

3. What is the distance between the points (2, 5) and (8, −3)? What is the length of the vector [6, −8]?

Show answer

Gaps: dx = 6, dy = −8. Squares: 36 and 64, sum 100, so the distance is √100 = 10. The vector [6, −8] is exactly those gaps measured from the origin, so its length is also 10. The minus sign on −8 disappears when it is squared.

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

Squaring turns a length into an area: signs vanish and big misses balloon; absolute value keeps just the length; and the square root of a sum of squares is a straight-line distance.
