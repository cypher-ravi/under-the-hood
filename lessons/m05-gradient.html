M05 · Partial derivatives and the gradient

A loss with two knobs is a landscape. Nudge one knob while holding the other still and you get that knob's slope, a partial derivative. Put every knob's slope in one list and you get the gradient: an arrow that points straight uphill. Training walks the other way.

a · Where this is usedThe AI lessons that need it

Read this before Lesson 08 · Gradient descent, which is one rule: step against the gradient, again and again. The same gradient fits the line in Lesson 09 · Linear regression (coming), and Lesson 23 · Backpropagation (coming) is a fast way to compute it for millions of knobs. It builds on the slopes of Lesson 05 · Derivatives and slopes and the five-flat loss of Lesson 07 · Loss functions.

b · Why this mattersTwo knobs, then two billion

Lesson 03 fitted the line ŷ = w·x + b to five flats by trying 24,381 settings of the two knobs and keeping the best. Lesson 05 showed a cheaper idea for one knob: measure the slope and move against it. Lesson 07 drew the loss as a bowl, but only while turning w with b held still.

A real model turns all its knobs at once. It needs, for every knob, one number: "if I move this knob a little, does the loss go up or down, and how fast?" Those numbers, stacked into one list, are the gradient. In PyTorch, after loss.backward(), each weight's .grad holds its entry of that list. This page builds the idea on our two knobs, with every number computed on the same five flats.

c · The intuitionHiking in fog

You are on a hillside in thick fog with a paper map. You can't see the valley, but you can test the ground. Take one pace east and check your altimeter: you climbed 3 metres. Go back, take one pace north: you dropped 1 metre. Those two numbers, one per direction, are partial derivatives. Together they tell you which way the ground rises fastest (mostly east, a little south), so the opposite way is the fastest way down.

The map helps too. Hiking maps draw contour lines: each line joins places at the same height. Walk along a line and you neither climb nor drop. Lines packed close together mean steep ground. For us, east and north become the knobs w and b, and height becomes the loss. The gradient is the "which way is up, and how steep" arrow, built from one test pace per knob.

d · How it actually worksEight steps, from one nudge to the gradient arrow

1. A function with two knobs

Our data is Lesson 03's five flats: x is the area in hundreds of sq ft (4, 6, 8, 10, 12) and y is the rent in thousands of rupees (14, 19, 22, 28, 31). The model guesses w·x + b, and the loss is the mean squared error from Lesson 07:

L(w, b) = (1/5) · Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²
L(w, b)
"L of w and b": the loss, one number, computed from two inputs. Like f(x) from M01, but with two inputs instead of one. The flats never change; only the knobs do.
xᵢ, yᵢ (the small i is a subscript)
The area and rent of flat number i. A subscript is a label, not a power: x₂ = 6 is the second flat's area. Σ means "add this up for i = 1 to 5" (M03).

Try w = 3, b = 0. The guesses are 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, the misses are −2, −1, 2, 2, 5, their squares add to 38, and L(3, 0) = 38 / 5 = 7.6. Try w = 2, b = 6: misses 0, −1, 0, −2, −1, squares add to 6, so L(2, 6) = 1.2. Every pair of knob values gives one loss, so you can lay them out as a table:

A table of 20 cells is Lesson 03's grid search in miniature. The rest of this page is about reading the slope at one cell instead of filling in all of them.

2. A partial derivative: nudge one knob, hold the other

Stand at (3, 0), where L = 7.6. Freeze b at 0 and nudge w from 3 to 3.1. The loss becomes 11.6. It rose by 4 over a nudge of 0.1, so the slope in the w direction is about 4 / 0.1 = 40. Smaller nudges, as in Lesson 05:

The w slopes settle on 32.8 (each one is 32.8 plus a leftover of 72·h), and the b slopes on 2.4 (leftover h). These two limits are the partial derivatives at (3, 0).

∂L/∂w, read "partial L by partial w"
The slope of L when only w moves and every other knob is held still. The curly ∂ replaces Lesson 05's d to warn you that there are other inputs, and they are frozen. So ∂L/∂w = 32.8 and ∂L/∂b = 2.4 at (3, 0).

3. The rules: treat the frozen knob as a plain number

Nudging works, but rules are faster. Expand the loss for one flat: (w·x + b − y)² multiplies out to x²·w² + 2x·wb + b² − 2xy·w − 2y·b + y². Average that over the five flats (Σx² = 360, Σx = 40, Σxy = 998, Σy = 114, Σy² = 2,786, each divided by 5) and the whole loss is six simple terms:

L(w, b) = 72w² + 16wb + b² − 399.2w − 45.6b + 557.2

Check it: at (3, 0) that is 648 + 0 + 0 − 1197.6 − 0 + 557.2 = 7.6. Now take the slope along w. Since b is frozen, it is just a number, like 5. So 16wb is "16b times w", whose slope is 16b, and terms with no w at all (b², −45.6b, 557.2) never move, so their slope is 0. Lesson 05's rules do the rest (72w² → 144w).

The same rules applied flat by flat give a shortcut you'll see in code: ∂L/∂w = (2/5)·Σ errorᵢ·xᵢ and ∂L/∂b = (2/5)·Σ errorᵢ, where errorᵢ = w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ. At (3, 0) the errors are −2, −1, 2, 2, 5, so Σ error·x = −8 − 6 + 16 + 20 + 60 = 82 and (2/5)·82 = 32.8; Σ error = 6 and (2/5)·6 = 2.4. Each flat pushes on w in proportion to its own miss times its own size.

4. The gradient: all the partials in one list

Write the two partial derivatives side by side and you have a vector (Lesson 04): a list of numbers that you can draw as an arrow.

∇L(w, b) = [ ∂L/∂w , ∂L/∂b ]
∇L, read "the gradient of L" (the symbol ∇ is called "nabla" or "del")
The list of partial derivatives, one per knob, in the same order as the knobs. With two knobs it has two entries; for a model with a billion weights it has a billion entries, one slope per weight.
|∇L|, its length
Pythagoras (M02): √(32.8² + 2.4²) = √1081.6 = 32.89. Step 6 shows what this length means.

At (3, 0): ∇L = [32.8, 2.4]. At (2, 6): [−15.2, −1.6]. At (2.15, 5.6), the best line from Lesson 03: [0, 0]. The gradient is itself a function: every point of the landscape gets its own arrow.

5. Contour lines: a hiking map of the loss

The table in step 1 shows only 20 points. A contour map shows all of them at once: pick a loss value, say 5, and draw a line through every (w, b) whose loss is exactly 5. Do the same for 1, 2, 10, 20, 40. The lines nest around the lowest point like the rings of a hill on a hiking map, except this is a valley.

Two things to read off. The rings are long and thin: the valley is a narrow canyon. That is because the knobs can trade off: raise w by 0.1 and the average guess rises by 0.8 (the average area is 8), which lowering b by 0.8 cancels. So walking along the canyon floor barely changes the loss, while stepping across it changes it fast. And the rings are closer together on the steep sides: from 20 to 40 is a short walk across the canyon, a long one along it.

6. The gradient points the steepest way uphill

Take a small step that changes w by Δw and b by Δb (Δ, "delta", means "a change in"). Do it in two legs: first w, which changes the loss by about ∂L/∂w · Δw; then b, about ∂L/∂b · Δb. For tiny steps the legs just add:

change in L ≈ ∂L/∂w · Δw + ∂L/∂b · Δb

From (3, 0) with Δw = Δb = 0.01: 32.8 × 0.01 + 2.4 × 0.01 = 0.352. The true change is 0.3609, close, and closer for smaller steps. The right side is the dot product of the gradient with the step. Lesson 04 showed that a dot product equals length × length × cos(angle), and the cosine is biggest (1) when the two arrows point the same way. So among all steps of the same length, the one along the gradient climbs fastest, and its slope is exactly |∇L|. The step along −∇L drops fastest. Try it on the dial:

7. The gradient crosses contour lines at a right angle

Walk along a contour line and the loss doesn't change: that is what the line means. So the slope along the contour is 0. From step 6 the slope in any direction is |∇L| × cos(angle to the gradient), and that is 0 only when the angle is 90°. So the contour line runs at a right angle to the gradient, wherever you stand. Uphill, downhill and "along the line" form a perfect cross.

A practical warning: the right angle is only visible when both axes use the same scale. The map in step 5 stretches b to fit, which would bend the angle on the page. The section e lab uses equal scales on purpose.

8. Length is steepness, and at the bottom both partials are 0

The gradient's length is how steep the ground is: 32.89 at (3, 0), 15.28 at (2, 6), which is already much flatter. At the very bottom of a smooth valley the ground is flat in every direction, so both partial derivatives are 0. Set the step 3 rules to 0:

144w + 16b − 399.2 = 0 and 16w + 2b − 45.6 = 0

The second says b = 22.8 − 8w. Put that into the first: 144w + 364.8 − 128w = 399.2, so 16w = 34.4, w = 2.15, and b = 22.8 − 17.2 = 5.6. That is Lesson 03's best line, ŷ = 2.15x + 5.6 with loss 0.38, found with two lines of algebra instead of 24,381 tries. In words, the two equations say: the errors add up to 0 (∂L/∂b = 0), and the errors times the areas add up to 0 (∂L/∂w = 0).

For a straight line, solving the equations is easy. For a neural network they can't be solved by hand, so Lesson 08 takes the other route: start anywhere and keep stepping along −∇L until the arrow shrinks to nothing.

f · Worked exampleBy hand, then in Python

The point (2, 6), Lesson 03's second guess. Guesses 2x + 6: 14, 18, 22, 26, 30. Errors (guess − truth): 0, −1, 0, −2, −1.

  • Loss: (0 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 1) / 5 = 1.2.
  • ∂L/∂w with the shortcut: Σ error·x = 0 − 6 + 0 − 20 − 12 = −38, so (2/5) × (−38) = −15.2. With the six-term rule: 144·2 + 16·6 − 399.2 = 288 + 96 − 399.2 = −15.2.
  • ∂L/∂b: Σ error = −4, so (2/5) × (−4) = −1.6. Rule: 16·2 + 2·6 − 45.6 = −1.6.
  • Nudged check: L(2.001, 6) = 1.184872, so (1.184872 − 1.2) / 0.001 = −15.128; L(2, 6.001) = 1.198401, so the b slope is −1.599.
  • Gradient [−15.2, −1.6], length √(231.04 + 2.56) = √233.6 = 15.28. Downhill is [15.2, 1.6]: raise w a lot, raise b a little.
  • Take a small step that way, 0.001 × the gradient: w = 2 + 0.0152 = 2.0152, b = 6 + 0.0016 = 6.0016. The loss drops from 1.2 to 0.9834.

The bottom. At (2.15, 5.6): 144·2.15 + 16·5.6 − 399.2 = 309.6 + 89.6 − 399.2 = 0 and 16·2.15 + 2·5.6 − 45.6 = 34.4 + 11.2 − 45.6 = 0.

import math

# Lesson 03's five flats: x = area (100s of sq ft), y = rent (Rs 1000s)
X = [4, 6, 8, 10, 12]
Y = [14, 19, 22, 28, 31]

def loss(w, b):
    """MSE of the line w*x + b on the five flats"""
    return sum((w * x + b - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(X, Y)) / len(X)

# 1. partial derivatives by nudging one knob (the other held still)
def nudge(w, b, h=0.001):
    d_w = (loss(w + h, b) - loss(w, b)) / h    # b held still
    d_b = (loss(w, b + h) - loss(w, b)) / h    # w held still
    return round(d_w, 3), round(d_b, 3)

# 2. the same from the rules: 2/n * sum(error * x) and 2/n * sum(error)
def gradient(w, b):
    errors = [w * x + b - y for x, y in zip(X, Y)]
    d_w = 2 / len(X) * sum(e * x for e, x in zip(errors, X))
    d_b = 2 / len(X) * sum(errors)
    return [round(d_w, 4) + 0.0, round(d_b, 4) + 0.0]   # + 0.0 turns -0.0 into 0.0

for w, b in [(3, 0), (2, 6), (2.15, 5.6)]:
    g = gradient(w, b)
    print((w, b), "L =", round(loss(w, b), 4), "nudged:", nudge(w, b),
          "gradient:", g, "length:", round(math.hypot(*g), 2))

# 3. a tiny step against the gradient lowers the loss
w, b = 2, 6
g = gradient(w, b)
step = 0.001
print(round(loss(w - step * g[0], b - step * g[1]), 4))

Python's output, exactly:

(3, 0) L = 7.6 nudged: (32.872, 2.401) gradient: [32.8, 2.4] length: 32.89
(2, 6) L = 1.2 nudged: (-15.128, -1.599) gradient: [-15.2, -1.6] length: 15.28
(2.15, 5.6) L = 0.38 nudged: (0.072, 0.001) gradient: [0.0, 0.0] length: 0.0
0.9834

Look at the last "nudged" pair: at the bottom the nudged w slope is 0.072, not 0. That is the 72·h leftover from step 2, with h = 0.001. The rule-based gradient has no leftover, which is one reason libraries compute exact gradients instead of nudging.

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"−∇L points straight at the lowest point."It points at the steepest way down from where you stand, which is across the contour line, not toward the bottom. In a narrow canyon those are very different directions.
"A partial derivative doesn't care about the other knob."It holds the other knob still while measuring, but where it is held changes the answer. ∂L/∂w = 144w + 16b − 399.2 has a b in it: at w = 3 it is 32.8 with b = 0 and 128.8 with b = 6. Change one knob and every partial can change.
"The gradient is the slope, one number."It is a list with one slope per knob, an arrow with a direction and a length. Its length is the steepness in the steepest direction (32.89 at (3, 0)), and its direction says which mix of knob changes gets there. A model with a billion weights has a billion-entry gradient on every step.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. At (2, 6), ∂L/∂b = −1.6. Should b go up or down to lower the loss, and about how much does the loss change if b rises by 0.01?Show answer

Up: the slope is negative, so increasing b lowers the loss. The change is about −1.6 × 0.01 = −0.016. The exact value is L(2, 6.01) − 1.2 = −0.0159.

2. At some point the gradient is [0, 3]. Which way is downhill, how steep is it, and what happens if you nudge only w?Show answer

Downhill is −∇L = [0, −3]: lower b, leave w. The steepness is √(0² + 3²) = 3. Nudging only w barely changes the loss, because ∂L/∂w = 0: you are walking along the contour line.

3. Using ∂L/∂w = 144w + 16b − 399.2 and ∂L/∂b = 16w + 2b − 45.6, what is the gradient at (2.5, 5.6)? Is that point left or right of the canyon floor?Show answer

144·2.5 + 16·5.6 − 399.2 = 360 + 89.6 − 399.2 = 50.4 and 16·2.5 + 2·5.6 − 45.6 = 40 + 11.2 − 45.6 = 5.6, so ∇L = [50.4, 5.6]. Uphill is "more w", so the floor is toward smaller w: the point is to the right of it (the best w is 2.15).

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

A partial derivative is one knob's slope with the others held still; the gradient lists them all as one arrow that points straight uphill, crosses the contour lines at right angles, and shrinks to zero at the bottom, so −∇L is the way down.
