06 · Probability basics for ML

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that says how often something happens out of all the times it could. It is the language a model uses to say how sure it is, and once you see it as counting a part out of a whole, every rule in this lesson becomes common sense.

Math you'll need
  • M03 · Summation (Σ) and averages

Rusty on any of these? Each is a short, from-scratch math lesson. Read it first, then come back.

a · Where we areFrom "which way to turn the knob" to "how sure is the answer"

Lesson 05 gave us slopes: for any loss, the derivative says which way to turn each knob. But we have not yet said what a classifier should output when the honest answer is "probably spam, but I can't be certain". Today we learn to say "probably" with numbers, so that Lesson 07 can measure how wrong such an answer is.

b · Why this mattersAlmost every model speaks in chances

A spam filter doesn't really say "spam". It says "spam with probability 0.92", and a separate rule decides what to do with that number. A weather app's "70% chance of rain", a fraud model's risk score and a chatbot picking its next word from a list of word probabilities all work the same way. If you can't read these numbers, you can't tell a confident model from a lucky one, and you can't understand the loss that trains it.

c · The intuitionA jar of marbles

Picture a jar with 20 marbles: 5 orange, 15 green. Shake it, close your eyes, pull one out. How likely is orange? You already know the answer without any formula: 5 out of 20, a quarter of the time. That is all a probability is: the share of the whole that matches what you care about.

Now someone tells you something extra: "the marble you pulled is one of the 4 with a scratch on it", and 3 of the scratched ones are orange. You forget the other 16 marbles. Your new "whole" is just the 4 scratched ones, and orange is now 3 out of 4. Learning a fact shrinks the whole you count over. That one move, called conditioning, is how a spam filter goes from "a quarter of all mail is spam" to "this email that says free is probably spam".

Swap marbles for emails, orange for spam and the scratch for the word "free", and you have the core of this lesson. The rest is giving names to these counts.

d · How it actually worksSix steps, from one die to a model's output

1. Outcomes, events and probability

Experiment and outcome
An experiment is anything with an uncertain result: rolling a die, picking an email from the inbox at random. Each possible result is an outcome. A die has six: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
Event
A group of outcomes you care about, given a name. "Roll an even number" is the event {2, 4, 6}. "The email is spam" is the event made of every spam email.
P(A), read "the probability of A"
A number from 0 to 1 saying what share of the time event A happens if you repeat the experiment many, many times. When every outcome is equally likely, you can get it by counting:
P(A) = (outcomes in A) / (all outcomes)

For a fair die, P(even) = 3 / 6 = 0.5 and P(roll a 6) = 1 / 6 ≈ 0.167. The "many times" part is important. Roll a die six times and you might not see a 6 at all. Roll it 6,000 times and very close to 1,000 of them will be 6s. The probability is what the running share settles towards, the same way Lesson 05's secant slopes settled towards the derivative. The lab in section e lets you watch that happen.

2. Four rules, all from counting

Every rule below follows from "part out of a whole". We'll use our inbox of 20 emails: 5 are spam, 4 contain the word "free", and 3 emails are both.

RuleIn symbolsWhy it's trueInbox example
Range0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1a part can't be smaller than nothing or bigger than the wholeP(spam) = 5/20 = 0.25
Totalall outcomes add up to 1every email is somewhereP(spam) + P(ok) = 0.25 + 0.75 = 1
ComplementP(not A) = 1 − P(A)what's left over from the wholeP(not spam) = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
ORP(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)adding counts both-at-once twice, so take it off once5/20 + 4/20 − 3/20 = 6/20 = 0.3

The OR check by plain counting: the 5 spam emails plus the 1 non-spam email that says "free" make 6 emails, and 6 / 20 = 0.3. The minus term matters: adding 5 and 4 would count the 3 "spam and free" emails twice.

3. AND, and what "independent" means

Flip a fair coin twice. The four outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT are equally likely, so P(two heads) = 1/4. Notice that 1/4 = 1/2 × 1/2. That shortcut works because the coin has no memory: the first flip does not change the second. Events like that are called independent, and only for them:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) (only if A and B are independent)

Spam and "free" are not independent. Multiplying would give 0.25 × 0.2 = 0.05, which is 1 email, but the real count is 3 emails, P(spam and free) = 3/20 = 0.15. The word "free" and spam go together more often than chance would suggest, and that link is exactly what a spam filter wants to learn.

4. Conditional probability: shrink the whole

Put the inbox into a small table, one row per label, one column per "free or not". This is called a contingency table, a table of counts.

says "free"no "free"total
spam325
ok11415
total41620

Question: an email says "free". How likely is it spam? Only look at the "says free" column. It has 4 emails, 3 of them spam, so the answer is 3 out of 4.

P(spam | free) = 3 / 4 = 0.75
P(A | B), read "the probability of A given B"
The share of B's outcomes that are also in A. The bar | means "given that we already know". B becomes the new whole.
The formula version
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). Same counting, with both counts divided by 20 first: 0.15 / 0.2 = 0.75. The 20s cancel, which is why counting inside the column gives the same answer.

Before you knew anything, the chance of spam was 0.25. After seeing "free" it is 0.75. A word tripled the odds, and this is the most basic way evidence moves a probability. Now flip the question around: of the spam emails, how many say "free"? Look along the spam row: 3 of 5, so P(free | spam) = 0.6. Same 3 emails on top, a different whole underneath, a different answer. The order inside P( | ) matters.

With this, the coin rule can be said precisely: A and B are independent when knowing B doesn't change the chance of A, that is, P(A | B) = P(A). For spam and "free", 0.75 ≠ 0.25, so they are not.

5. One gentle step of Bayes' rule

Often you know one direction and need the other. A filter's designers can measure P(free | spam) by reading through a pile of known spam, but in use they need P(spam | free). Both come from the same "both" count, P(spam and free), so you can go from one to the other:

P(spam | free) = P(free | spam) × P(spam) / P(free)

Check with our numbers: 0.6 × 0.25 / 0.2 = 0.15 / 0.2 = 0.75. That's the same 0.75 we counted. This is Bayes' rule. The top line rebuilds the "both" count; dividing by P(free) shrinks the whole to the "free" column. Nothing new happens, only the same table read in the other direction.

6. Distributions and the expected value

Sometimes the outcome is a number: the face of a die, or the "!" count of an email. A random variable, written with a capital like X, is that number before you know it. Its probability distribution is the full list of values it can take, each with its probability, and the probabilities must add up to 1.

Lessons 01 and 02 had 8 emails with "!" counts 0, 1, 0, 2, 3, 5, 1, 4. Pick one at random and let X be its count:

The bottom row leads to the expected value, also called the expectation or mean: multiply each value by its probability and add.

E[X] = Σ x · P(X = x) = 2
E[X], read "the expected value of X"
A probability-weighted average: values that happen more often count for more. Here it equals the plain average (16 bangs / 8 emails = 2) because every email was equally likely to be picked.
Σ, the capital Greek letter sigma
Short for "add up the following over every value". Here, over x = 0 to 5.

"Expected" doesn't mean "the value you'll see". A fair die has E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) × 1/6 = 3.5, and you will never roll a 3.5. It means the long-run average: roll many times and the average of your rolls settles near 3.5. Change the probabilities and the expectation moves with them. A loaded die that shows 6 half the time (and each other face 0.1 of the time) has E[X] = 0.1 × (1+2+3+4+5) + 0.5 × 6 = 4.5.

7. Why models output probabilities

A classifier's output is itself a distribution over the possible labels. For one email it might say [spam: 0.92, ok: 0.08]. For a photo, [cat: 0.70, dog: 0.20, bird: 0.10]. In both cases the numbers are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1, the same rules as above. Two reasons this beats a bare "spam":

  • It keeps the doubt. 0.92 and 0.51 both round to "spam", but you'd only want to auto-delete the first. Whoever uses the model can pick a threshold that fits the cost of each kind of mistake.
  • It can be scored smoothly. If the email really is spam, a model that said 0.92 was a little wrong and one that said 0.10 was very wrong. A plain yes/no throws that difference away. Probabilities keep it, and a loss can turn it into a number whose slope (Lesson 05) tells each knob how to move. That loss is Lesson 07.

Read 0.92 as a conditional probability: P(spam | this email's features) = 0.92, the model's estimate of what share of emails like this one are spam. Our 20-email table did the same by counting; a model has to estimate it for emails it has never seen.

f · Worked exampleBy hand, then in Python

The inbox. 20 emails. Spam: 5, all from unknown senders, 3 of them say "free". Ok: 15, of which 1 says "free" (a colleague offering free lunch, from a known sender), 9 more are from known senders, and 5 are from unknown senders (shops, banks, newsletters).

  • P(spam) = 5 / 20 = 0.25 and P(free) = 4 / 20 = 0.2
  • P(spam and free) = 3 / 20 = 0.15 (if they were independent it would be 0.25 × 0.2 = 0.05; it isn't)
  • P(spam or free) = 0.25 + 0.2 − 0.15 = 0.3, which is 6 emails
  • P(not spam) = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
  • P(spam | free) = 0.15 / 0.2 = 0.75, that is 3 of the 4 "free" emails
  • P(free | spam) = 0.15 / 0.25 = 0.6, that is 3 of the 5 spam emails
  • Bayes, the long way round: 0.6 × 0.25 / 0.2 = 0.75, the same answer

An expectation. "!" counts of the 8 emails from Lessons 01–02: 0, 1, 0, 2, 3, 5, 1, 4. The distribution is {0: 0.25, 1: 0.25, 2: 0.125, 3: 0.125, 4: 0.125, 5: 0.125}, which sums to 1, and E[X] = 0×0.25 + 1×0.25 + 2×0.125 + 3×0.125 + 4×0.125 + 5×0.125 = 0 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.375 + 0.5 + 0.625 = 2. For a fair die, E[X] = 21 / 6 = 3.5.

The same thing in plain Python. P does exactly what the inbox lab does: keep the emails where the condition holds, then take the share of those where the event holds.

# 20 emails: (is_spam, has_free, known_sender)
inbox = (
    [(1, 1, 0)] * 3 +   # spam, says "free", unknown sender
    [(1, 0, 0)] * 2 +   # spam, no "free", unknown sender
    [(0, 1, 1)] * 1 +   # ok, says "free", known sender
    [(0, 0, 1)] * 9 +   # ok, no "free", known sender
    [(0, 0, 0)] * 5     # ok, no "free", unknown sender
)
n = len(inbox)

def P(event, given=lambda e: True):
    """count the emails where `given` holds, then the share of those where `event` holds"""
    pool = [e for e in inbox if given(e)]
    return sum(1 for e in pool if event(e)) / len(pool)

spam = lambda e: e[0] == 1
free = lambda e: e[1] == 1

print(n, P(spam), P(free))                      # 20 0.25 0.2
print(P(lambda e: spam(e) and free(e)))         # 0.15   P(spam and free)
print(P(lambda e: spam(e) or free(e)))          # 0.3    P(spam or free)
print(1 - P(spam))                              # 0.75   P(not spam)
print(P(spam, given=free))                      # 0.75   P(spam | free)
print(P(free, given=spam))                      # 0.6    P(free | spam)
print(round(P(free, given=spam) * P(spam) / P(free), 4))   # 0.75   Bayes' rule

# expectation: "!" counts of the 8 emails from Lessons 01-02
bangs = [0, 1, 0, 2, 3, 5, 1, 4]
dist = {k: bangs.count(k) / len(bangs) for k in sorted(set(bangs))}
print(dist, sum(dist.values()))
# {0: 0.25, 1: 0.25, 2: 0.125, 3: 0.125, 4: 0.125, 5: 0.125} 1.0
print(sum(k * p for k, p in dist.items()))      # 2.0    expected value
print(sum(f * (1 / 6) for f in range(1, 7)))    # 3.5    fair die

Notice that P never uses a formula like P(A and B) / P(B). It just counts inside the smaller pool, and the formula falls out. Real ML libraries don't count tables of emails, but the numbers they produce mean exactly this.

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"P(spam | free) and P(free | spam) are the same thing."They share the same top count but divide by different wholes. Here they are 0.75 and 0.6, and they can be far apart. Most emails from your bank mention "account", yet most emails mentioning "account" are not from your bank. Mixing up the two directions is one of the most common reasoning errors, in ML and in medicine.
"After five heads in a row, tails is due."A fair coin has no memory; each flip is independent, so the next one is still 0.5. Long-run shares settle near 0.5 not because the coin makes up for past streaks, but because thousands of later flips swamp a short streak. In the dice lab, an early lopsided run never gets "corrected"; it just becomes a smaller and smaller part of the total.
"A model that says 0.92 is right 92% of the time on that email."For one email the answer is simply spam or not. 0.92 is a claim about emails like it: of all emails the model scores near 0.92, about 92% should be spam. A model whose numbers live up to that is called well calibrated. Many models are not, so a high number is a hint, not a guarantee.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. Roll a fair die once. What is the probability of rolling 5 or more? What about less than 5?

Show answer

"5 or more" is {5, 6}, 2 of 6 outcomes, so 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333. "Less than 5" is its complement: 1 − 1/3 = 2/3 ≈ 0.667. Counting {1, 2, 3, 4} gives the same 4/6.

2. In the 20-email inbox, what is P(spam | unknown sender)? And P(unknown sender | spam)?

Show answer

There are 10 unknown senders (5 spam + 5 ok), so P(spam | unknown) = 5/10 = 0.5. All 5 spam emails came from unknown senders, so P(unknown | spam) = 5/5 = 1. Same top count, different wholes, very different answers. Check both in the inbox lab.

3. The number of spam emails arriving in a minute is 0 with probability 0.5, 1 with probability 0.3 and 2 with probability 0.2. Is this a valid distribution, and what is the expected number per minute?

Show answer

Valid: every probability is between 0 and 1, and 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 1. E[X] = 0×0.5 + 1×0.3 + 2×0.2 = 0.7 spam emails per minute, about 42 an hour. You will never get 0.7 in one minute; it is the long-run average.

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

A probability is a part divided by a whole; knowing something new just shrinks the whole you count over, and a model's "0.92 spam" is that same kind of number.
