07 · Loss functions: measuring how wrong a model is

A loss function takes everything a model got right and wrong on a dataset and boils it down to one number, where smaller means better. Training is nothing more than turning the knobs to make that number small, so the way you score mistakes decides what the model learns.

Math you'll need
  • M02 · Squares, square roots and absolute value
  • M03 · Summation (Σ) and averages
  • M04 · Exponents and logarithms

Rusty on any of these? Each is a short, from-scratch math lesson. Read it first, then come back.

a · Where we arePutting a number on "wrong"

Lesson 03 scored a line on five flats with the "total squared error" and promised to explain that choice later. Lesson 05 walked a knob downhill on a loss for one flat. Lesson 06 taught models to answer with probabilities like "spam, 0.92". Today we pull those threads together: what a loss is, the three losses you will meet everywhere, and why each one is shaped the way it is.

b · Why this mattersThe loss is the only thing training sees

A model never sees your goal ("price flats well", "catch spam"). It only sees the loss, one number per training step, and it changes its knobs to push that number down. If the loss rewards the wrong thing, the model will learn the wrong thing very efficiently. When a PyTorch script writes loss = nn.MSELoss()(pred, y) or nn.CrossEntropyLoss(), that one line decides which mistakes the model takes seriously. As an engineer, choosing and reading the loss is the closest you get to telling the model what you want.

c · The intuitionDarts and a scorecard

You throw five darts at a bullseye. How good was the round? You could count hits, but a dart that missed by a millimetre and one that hit the wall would both count as "miss". A fairer scorecard measures how far each dart landed from the centre and adds those distances up. Lower is better; zero means every dart hit dead centre.

Now the design choices appear. Should one dart that hit the wall cost the same as five darts that were each a little off? Some coaches would say "the wild throw is the real problem, punish it hard". Others would say "one bad throw shouldn't ruin a good round". Both are reasonable, and they lead to different scorecards.

A loss function is that scorecard for a model. Each prediction is a dart, the true answer is the bullseye, and the rule for turning "how far off" into points is the part you get to choose.

d · How it actually worksSeven steps, from one miss to choosing a loss

1. One error per example

Take one example: a flat with true rent y and the model's guess ŷ ("y-hat", Lesson 03). The error on that example is the gap between them:

error = ŷ − y (guess minus truth)
Error (also: miss, residual)
Positive means the guess was too high, negative means too low, zero means spot on. Statistics books call it the residual and often write it the other way round, y − ŷ. The sign flips, but once we square it or take its size the two versions give the same loss.

Our data is Lesson 03's five flats, with x the area in hundreds of sq ft and y the rent in thousands of rupees. Try the line ŷ = 3x (knobs w = 3, b = 0):

Five errors, five numbers. That's a lot to look at. To compare two lines, or to let a computer pick the better one, we need to squeeze them into one number. Every loss function is a recipe for that squeeze.

2. Why we can't just add the errors

The obvious recipe is to add them up: −2 − 1 + 2 + 2 + 5 = 6. The trouble shows up with a lazy model that ignores area and guesses the average rent, 22.8, for every flat:

The sum is 8.8 + 3.8 + 0.8 − 5.2 − 8.2 = 0. A score of zero for a model that is off by ₹8,800 on two flats is useless. Too-high and too-low errors cancel each other, and the average guess always cancels perfectly (that's what an average is, see M03).

The fix is to make every error count as a cost, never a credit, before adding. There are two common ways to turn a signed number into a positive "size": take its absolute value or square it (M02 covers both). Each one gives a famous loss.

3. Mean absolute error (MAE)

Drop the sign of each error, add the sizes, and divide by how many examples there are:

MAE = (1/n) · Σ |ŷᵢ − yᵢ|
|e|, "the absolute value of e"
The size of e with its sign dropped: |−2| = 2, |5| = 5. Think of it as distance from zero.
n, i and Σ
n is the number of examples (5 flats). i names one example: yᵢ is the rent of flat i. Σ means "add up the following for i = 1 to n", a for-loop on paper.
Why divide by n
So the loss is an average per example. Then a dataset of 5 flats and one of 5,000 give numbers on the same scale.

For ŷ = 3x: sizes 2, 1, 2, 2, 5, total 12, so MAE = 12 / 5 = 2.4. In plain words: on average this line is off by ₹2,400. MAE is in the same units as the thing you're predicting, which makes it easy to explain to anyone. The lazy 22.8 model, which the plain sum called perfect, gets MAE = 26.8 / 5 = 5.36: more than twice as bad, as it should be.

4. Mean squared error (MSE)

Instead of dropping the sign, square each error. A square is never negative, so nothing cancels:

MSE = (1/n) · Σ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)²

For ŷ = 3x: squares 4, 1, 4, 4, 25, total 38 (Lesson 03's "total squared error"), so MSE = 38 / 5 = 7.6. Look at flat E: its error of 5 contributes 25 of the 38, two thirds of the whole loss. That is the point of squaring:

So MSE is the coach who says "the wild throw is the real problem". A model trained on it works hardest on its biggest misses. Two side effects: the units are now "rupees squared", which mean nothing to a human, so people often report the square root, the RMSE (root mean squared error): √7.6 ≈ 2.76, back in thousands of rupees. And the U is smooth at the bottom, with no sharp corner like the V, so its slope (Lesson 05) is easy to work with everywhere. That's a big reason MSE is the default for predicting numbers.

5. The loss is a function of the knobs

Here is the shift in viewpoint that makes training possible. The data is fixed: five flats, never changing. The only things that can change are the knobs w and b. So the loss is really a function of the knobs:

L(w, b) = (1/5) · Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²

Give it a knob setting, it gives back one number. Hold b at 5.6 and turn w, and you trace out a curve:

This is the landscape Lesson 05 walked on. There, the loss was (8w − 22)² for flat C alone, a single bowl. Here it is the average of five such bowls, one per flat, and the average of bowls is still a bowl. Training means finding the bottom of this bowl: the knob setting with the smallest loss. Lesson 08 does it by following the slope downhill, step after step, instead of trying every setting.

6. Classification: log loss

For a spam filter the answer is a label, not a number, and the model outputs a probability (Lesson 06). An "error" like 0.92 − 1 is possible, but there is a better question to ask: how much probability did the model give to what actually happened? Call that p. The cost is

cost = −ln p
ln, the natural logarithm
ln p answers "what power of e ≈ 2.718 gives p?" (M04). For p between 0 and 1 it is 0 or negative, so the minus sign turns it into a cost of 0 or more.
p, the probability given to the truth
If the email is spam, p = P(spam). If it is ok, p = 1 − P(spam). The model is never "partly right"; it gets credit for the probability it put on the truth.

Averaging this cost over all examples gives log loss, also called binary cross-entropy. Score four emails. A filter that has learned Lesson 06's table says 0.75 for any email with "free" (that's P(spam | free)) and 0.25 for an ordinary one:

7. Choosing a loss

Two questions settle most cases.

Is the answer a number or a label? For numbers (rent, temperature, delivery time), use MSE or MAE. For labels with probabilities (spam or not, cat or dog), use log loss. Why not MSE on probabilities? Its cost can never be more than 1: if the truth is spam and the model said 0.01, the squared error is (1 − 0.01)² = 0.9801, while log loss is −ln 0.01 = 4.6052. MSE barely notices a confident blunder; log loss sounds the alarm.

How should big mistakes count? Suppose someone types ₹40,000 instead of ₹20,000 for a sixth flat, F, of 600 sq ft. Let the computer find the best line under each loss:

Without F, both losses pick almost the same line (2.15x + 5.6 for MSE, 2.1x + 5.8 for MAE). With F, MSE bends to cut that one huge squared error, and its price for a 900 sq ft flat jumps from 24.95 to 27.4, while MAE's only moves from 24.7 to 25.0. So: use MAE when you expect junk or rare wild values you don't want to chase. Use MSE when big misses really are worse (a delivery that's an hour late is much worse than six 10-minute delays) and the data is clean.

The same in plain Python, including the search for the best line under each loss with and without the typo flat (the same grid as Lesson 03):

import math

# the five flats from Lesson 03: x = area (100s of sq ft), y = rent (Rs 1000s)
X = [4, 6, 8, 10, 12]
Y = [14, 19, 22, 28, 31]

def errors(w, b, X=X, Y=Y):
    """guess minus truth, one per flat"""
    return [w * x + b - y for x, y in zip(X, Y)]

def mae(w, b, X=X, Y=Y):
    e = errors(w, b, X, Y)
    return sum(abs(t) for t in e) / len(e)

def mse(w, b, X=X, Y=Y):
    e = errors(w, b, X, Y)
    return sum(t * t for t in e) / len(e)

print(errors(3, 0))                                  # [-2, -1, 2, 2, 5]
print(sum(errors(3, 0)), mae(3, 0), mse(3, 0))       # 6 2.4 7.6
print(round(sum(errors(0, 22.8)), 4))                # 0.0   errors cancel
print(round(mae(0, 22.8), 4), round(mse(0, 22.8), 4))  # 5.36 37.36
print(mae(2, 6), mse(2, 6))                          # 0.8 1.2
print(round(mse(2.15, 5.6), 4), round(math.sqrt(mse(3, 0)), 4))  # 0.38 2.7568

# the loss as a function of the knob w (b held at 5.6): a bowl
for w in [1.5, 2, 2.15, 2.5, 3]:
    print(w, round(mse(w, 5.6), 4))

# training = find the knobs with the lowest loss (same grid as Lesson 03)
def fit(loss, X, Y):
    best = None
    for i in range(81):                 # w = 0.00 ... 4.00
        for j in range(-100, 201):      # b = -10.0 ... 20.0
            w, b = i * 0.05, j / 10
            e = loss(w, b, X, Y)
            if best is None or e < best[0] - 1e-12:
                best = (e, w, b)
    return best

# flat F: 600 sq ft typed in at Rs 40,000 by mistake
X6, Y6 = X + [6], Y + [40]
for name, loss in [("MSE", mse), ("MAE", mae)]:
    for data in [(X, Y), (X6, Y6)]:
        e, w, b = fit(loss, *data)
        print(name, len(data[0]), "flats: w =", round(w, 2), "b =", round(b, 1),
              "-> 900 sq ft:", round(w * 9 + b, 2))

# classification: the model's P(spam) for four emails, and the truth
emails = [(1, 0.92), (0, 0.25), (1, 0.75), (0, 0.75)]   # (is_spam, P(spam))

def log_loss(y, p):
    """-ln of the probability the model gave to the true answer"""
    return -math.log(p if y == 1 else 1 - p)

costs = [log_loss(y, p) for y, p in emails]
print([round(c, 4) for c in costs])        # one cost per email
print(round(sum(costs) / len(costs), 4))   # average log loss
print(round(log_loss(1, 0.01), 4), round((1 - 0.01) ** 2, 4))  # confidently wrong

g · Common misconceptionsThree things that sound right but aren't

"A loss of 0.38 is good."A loss number means nothing on its own. MSE is in squared units, so change rent from thousands of rupees to rupees and the same line's MSE becomes 380,000. Losses are for comparing settings of the same model on the same data, or for watching a number fall during training.
"The loss and the metric you report are the same thing."Often they differ on purpose. A spam filter is trained on log loss because it is smooth and has useful slopes, but its team reports accuracy or "spam caught" because people understand those. Accuracy is a bad training signal: nudging a knob a tiny bit usually changes no decisions, so its slope is zero almost everywhere.
"MSE is the correct loss; MAE is a cruder version."Neither is more correct. They encode different opinions about big mistakes. With the typo flat, MSE's "best" line got worse on every real flat. The right loss is the one whose idea of "bad" matches yours.

h · Check yourselfThree quick questions

1. A model predicts 3 delivery times with errors +4, −4 and 0 minutes. What are the sum of errors, the MAE and the MSE?Show answer

Sum: 4 − 4 + 0 = 0, which wrongly suggests a perfect model. MAE: (4 + 4 + 0) / 3 ≈ 2.67 minutes. MSE: (16 + 16 + 0) / 3 ≈ 10.67 minutes squared (RMSE ≈ 3.27 minutes).

2. Two models see an ok email. Model A says P(spam) = 0.6, model B says P(spam) = 0.99. Both are wrong at a 0.5 cut-off. What does log loss charge each?Show answer

The probability given to the truth (ok) is 1 − P(spam). A: −ln 0.4 ≈ 0.9163. B: −ln 0.01 ≈ 4.6052, five times more. Accuracy counts them as the same single mistake; log loss punishes B's confidence.

3. You are predicting house prices from a scraped dataset where a few rows have prices typed with an extra zero. Which loss would you start with, and why?Show answer

MAE (or a blend that behaves like it for big errors). A price ten times too high gives an error so large that its square would dominate MSE, dragging the line toward the bad rows, just like flat F. MAE charges it in proportion, so the clean rows still decide the fit. Better still: find and fix the bad rows.

i · The one-line takeawayIf you remember one thing

A loss function turns all of a model's mistakes into one number to minimize, and how it scores each mistake (absolute, squared, or −ln of the probability on the truth) decides what the model cares about.
